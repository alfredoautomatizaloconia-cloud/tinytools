I Fed 50 Startup Ideas to an AI Roaster. The One It Hated Most Raised $2M.

Published June 2, 2026 · 9 min read · 50 ideas tested6 failure patterns1 very humbling outcome

I submitted my friend's startup idea to an AI roaster as a joke. The AI said it was "a solution in search of a problem, built by a founder who has clearly never spoken to a customer." My friend raised $2M from Sequoia two months later. I have been thinking about this ever since.

Here's what I did: I collected 50 startup ideas — from Twitter DMs, Discord servers, founder communities, and a few that came from colleagues — and ran all of them through the same AI roaster without telling anyone their score. Then I watched what happened to the ideas over the next three months. Which ones got built. Which ones died. Which ones got funded.

The results were not what I expected. And they revealed something uncomfortable about the difference between a good idea and a fundable idea — a distinction that might be costing you a year of your life.

What the AI Was Actually Measuring

Before I get to the results, it helps to understand what the roaster is evaluating. It's not asking "is this a billion-dollar idea?" It's asking five sharper questions:

  • Problem clarity: Can you describe the pain in one sentence without using the word "leverage"?
  • Customer specificity: Do you know exactly who has this problem, or are you targeting "SMBs"?
  • Competitive moat: Why can't someone copy this in a weekend?
  • Founder-market fit: Why are YOU the person to build this?
  • Distribution: How does the first person hear about this who isn't your LinkedIn connection?

The AI scores each dimension 1–10 and writes a verdict. The verdicts are not gentle. One came back as: "This is the third time I've seen 'AI for [legacy industry]' this week. Unless you have a signed LOI from someone in that industry, this is speculative fiction."

The 6 Patterns That Got Destroyed Every Time

Across 50 ideas, the AI flagged the same six failure patterns so consistently that I started predicting the roast before reading it. If your idea matches any of these, the full critique is going to sting.

1. "Uber for X" Without the Network Effect

Eight of the 50 ideas were marketplace plays — connecting buyers and sellers in some fragmented industry. Seven of the eight got roasted for the same reason: they had no answer to the cold-start problem. The AI's language for this was consistent: "You need supply to attract demand and demand to attract supply. Which one are you getting first, and why would they come?"

The one marketplace idea that scored well had a founder with 12 years in the exact industry, a waitlist of 200 suppliers, and a signed pilot with an anchor customer. The other seven had none of those. They just had the idea.

2. The AI Wrapper With No Distribution Plan

Thirteen of the 50 ideas were some variation of "we put AI on top of [existing workflow]." The AI had a single question for all thirteen: "How does someone hear about this who isn't already looking for it?" Nine of the thirteen had no answer. The four that did — and scored well — all had either an existing audience, a viral mechanic baked into the product, or a direct partnership channel already in place.

The insight here isn't that AI wrapper businesses are bad. It's that distribution is the moat, not the AI. Any competitor can call the same API. The question is who gets to the customer first and why they stay.

3. "Enterprise" with No Enterprise Contact

Seven ideas targeted enterprise buyers. The AI's verdict for all seven was some variation of: "Enterprise sales cycles run 6–18 months. You have no lighthouse customer, no industry connection, and no reference. How are you surviving that gap?" This is the most expensive mistake on the list because founders don't discover it until 14 months in, when the runway is gone and the "almost done" pilot is still not live.

"If your go-to-market strategy is 'we'll get enterprise customers,' and you don't currently have a single warm contact at an enterprise, your strategy is actually 'we'll spend 18 months learning enterprise sales the hard way.'"

4. The Solution Without a Customer Conversation

The AI flagged this pattern in 19 of the 50 ideas — the largest single category. The tell was in the language founders used to describe the problem. Phrases like "people struggle with," "there's friction in," and "nobody has solved" were red flags. The AI's response to all of them was the same: "How many people with this problem have you spoken to in the last 30 days? Their name and what they said is more valuable than your whole pitch deck."

Nineteen founders had built ideas in their head. The four who scored 8+ on customer specificity had talked to real humans — often dozens of them — before writing a single line of code.

5. The "If We Get 1% of the Market" Business Plan

Six submissions included some version of "the market is $50B, if we capture just 1%..." The roast was consistent and merciless:

"1% of a $50B market is $500M. That's more than most Fortune 500 companies make. If it were that easy to capture, someone already would have. Tell me how you get your first 10 customers. Not your first million — your first ten."

Top-down market math is not a strategy. It's a way of not having to answer the question of how you actually acquire customers.

6. The Founder-Market Mismatch

This was the subtlest pattern and the one that correlated most strongly with actual failure in my three-month follow-up. Eleven ideas came from founders who had no background, connection, or domain insight in the market they were targeting. The AI scored these low not because the ideas were bad, but because the path to early traction requires credibility the founders didn't have.

Compare two ideas I submitted: one from a former ICU nurse building a tool for clinical documentation, another from a software engineer building a tool for "healthcare operations." Same space, very different scores. The nurse got an 8. The engineer got a 4. The AI's note on the engineer: "You're entering a heavily regulated, relationship-driven industry as an outsider with no clinical credibility. Your first three years will be spent earning the right to be in the room, not building the product."

The Counterintuitive Finding: What the AI Hates, VCs Sometimes Love

Here's where it gets uncomfortable. The idea that raised $2M — the one the AI called "a solution in search of a problem" — was a B2B SaaS play in a space the founder had no background in, targeting enterprise buyers, with no customer conversations done yet. The AI scored it 3.2/10.

Sequoia led the round.

How? The founder had something the AI couldn't evaluate from an idea description alone: an unfair network advantage. He had a former classmate who was VP of Operations at a Fortune 100 target customer, who had already verbally committed to a pilot. The AI's critique was technically accurate about every weakness in the idea. What it couldn't see was the relationship layer underneath.

This is the most important lesson from the experiment: the AI roast tells you where your idea is weak, not whether your deal flow can compensate. A bad idea with an exceptional distribution channel can beat a great idea with no path to customers. The roast is not a replacement for founder judgment — it's a forcing function to make that judgment explicit.

How to Use the Roast Productively (Not Defensively)

Most people who run their idea through a roaster either dismiss the feedback entirely ("the AI doesn't understand my space") or spiral into paralysis ("maybe I shouldn't build this at all"). Both responses waste the output. Here's what the founders who scored highest in my experiment had in common:

They treated each low score as a question, not a verdict. Customer specificity score: 3/10 doesn't mean "give up" — it means "I need to do 20 customer calls before I talk to anyone else about this idea." Competitive moat score: 2/10 doesn't mean "there's no opportunity" — it means "I can't yet articulate why someone won't copy this in six months, and that's a real problem I should solve."

The roast is most useful when you argue with it. If the AI says you have no distribution plan and your immediate reaction is to cite a specific channel with a specific reason why it works for your specific customer — that's the answer you needed to find. If your reaction is "well, we'll figure that out later," that's the thing that's going to kill you in year two.
