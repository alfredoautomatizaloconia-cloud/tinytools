Who Does This Actually Hit?

The short answer: anyone publishing AI-generated content that reaches EU residents. That's not a narrow slice of the internet.

The regulation uses the phrase "intended to interact with natural persons" — which is basically every piece of content on the public web. If a person in Germany, France, or Spain might read your blog post, see your Instagram image, or watch your YouTube video, Article 50 applies to you.

Specifically, disclosure is required for:

  • AI-generated or AI-manipulated images published publicly
  • AI-generated audio content (podcasts, voiceovers, ads)
  • AI-generated video (promotional content, explainers, deepfakes)
  • AI-generated text intended to inform or persuade (blog posts, ad copy, product descriptions)
  • AI-generated synthetic media designed to appear realistic

Note what's not exempt: your industry, your company size, your jurisdiction. A solo creator in Austin with 3,000 Instagram followers who uses Midjourney for their posts is technically in scope if any of those followers are in the EU.

What Does "Compliant" Actually Look Like?

This is where the regulation gets technical — and where most explainer articles wave their hands and say "consult a lawyer." I'm going to be more specific.

Article 50 requires disclosures to be:

1. Machine-readable (not just human-visible)

A small "AI-generated" text label on your image doesn't cut it. The requirement is for embedded metadata or structured schema that automated systems can detect. That means either C2PA cryptographic provenance data embedded in image/video files, or JSON-LD schema markup in your HTML that identifies the content as AI-generated. A visual watermark is good but it's not sufficient on its own.

2. Clear and distinguishable to users

The label must be visible to humans in a way that doesn't require effort to find. Hidden in footer text? Buried in metadata only? Not compliant. The spirit of the requirement is that an ordinary person encountering your content should immediately understand it was AI-generated.

3. Present at the point of interaction

You can't link to a disclosure page. The label needs to be on or immediately adjacent to the content itself — in the image, on the video, or in the HTML document containing the text.

"The obligation to label applies to any natural or legal person deploying AI systems that generate or manipulate content... regardless of whether that content constitutes 'high-risk' AI under other articles of the Act."
— EU AI Act Article 50, Recital 134

The Four Disclosure Formats You Need to Know

Different content types require different disclosure approaches. Here's what each one looks like in practice:

Text content (blog posts, ad copy, product descriptions)

The minimum viable disclosure is a JSON-LD Article schema with "creator": {"@type": "SoftwareApplication"} identifying the AI system. For higher-confidence compliance, you want an HTML badge element rendered above or below the content with appropriate ARIA labels for accessibility.

Images

C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) manifest embedded in the file's metadata is the gold standard. For platforms that strip EXIF data (most social networks), you need a visible overlay or caption-level disclosure. The spec recommends both.

Audio

XMP metadata in the file header plus an audible disclosure at the start of the recording ("This content was created with AI assistance") is the current best practice. For short-form clips under 60 seconds, the audible disclosure can be replaced with platform-level labeling if the platform itself supports C2PA verification.

Video and deepfakes

The most stringent requirements apply here. Realistic synthetic video of real people (even with consent) requires C2PA provenance, a visible persistent overlay during playback, and platform-level flagging where available. "Deepfake" content — where a real person's likeness is generated or modified — requires explicit disclosure even in satirical contexts.

Why Most "Compliance" Checklists Miss the Point

I've seen a lot of EU AI Act guides published by agencies and law firms over the past year. Most of them focus on the high-risk AI classification system (Annex III) because that's where the regulatory drama is: facial recognition, credit scoring, hiring algorithms. The stuff that makes headlines.

But Article 50 is different. It's not about how AI makes decisions. It's about labeling AI output — and it applies universally, regardless of risk classification. A generative AI tool that writes product descriptions for your e-commerce store is zero-risk under Annex III, but every piece of copy it produces requires Article 50 disclosure.

The practical implication: your compliance team may have already run a full EU AI Act risk assessment and concluded your tools are low-risk, while unknowingly leaving your content team out of scope. That's the gap most companies have right now.

Step 3: Update your content templates

Step 4: Decide on retroactive disclosure

Here's the uncomfortable question: what do you do with the AI content you've already published? The Act doesn't have explicit retroactive enforcement provisions, but best practice is to add disclosures to high-traffic or high-value content as you update it. Prioritize content that could be mistaken for non-AI human work.

Step 5: Document your compliance workflow

Market supervisory authorities will want to see evidence of a good-faith compliance effort. Keep records of when you implemented disclosures and what process you follow. This is your paper trail if there's ever an investigation.

  • Content publishing templates updated to include disclosures automatically
  • Compliance workflow documented with implementation dates

The Honest Take: Will This Actually Be Enforced?

The real question everyone is too nervous to ask in compliance meetings.

Short answer: yes, eventually, and selectively. Enforcement of novel regulations typically starts with high-profile cases — large companies or egregious violations that make good examples. Small creators publishing mislabeled AI images are unlikely to face fines in 2026. But the companies that assume this is theoretical are the ones who get caught flat-footed when enforcement ramps up.

More practically: the disclosure requirement will increasingly be enforced by platforms rather than regulators. Instagram, LinkedIn, and YouTube are all actively building AI content detection systems and will begin requiring disclosure labels (or adding them automatically) regardless of your legal obligations. If you're already compliant when they roll that out, you're ahead. If you're not, you'll either be flagged or retrofitted — on their timeline, not yours.
