How to Extract a Color Palette from a Logo or Photo in 2026

Updated May 10, 2026 · 8 min read · By the TinyTools team

You found the perfect reference — a competitor's landing page, a film still, a photo of your favorite cafe, the logo your designer mocked up six months ago — and now you need the colors. Not vibes. Real hex codes that survive a Tailwind config, a contrast audit, and the harsh fluorescent reality of someone screenshotting your dashboard onto a 2018 Dell monitor.

The good news: extracting a usable palette from an image in 2026 takes about five minutes if you know what to look for. The bad news: most "color extractor" tools spit out twelve nearly identical mid-tone blues and call it a palette, which is why you keep ending up with brand systems that feel muddy. Pick the right source image first

The single biggest mistake people make is extracting from the wrong file. A photo and a logo need different treatment, and so does a screenshot of a finished UI vs. a marketing image with film grain over it. Before you upload anything, classify it.

Vector logos (SVG, AI, EPS)

If your logo is vector, skip image extraction entirely. Open the SVG in a text editor and grep for fill= and stop-color=. You'll have the exact hex values the designer used in under thirty seconds, with zero rounding error from JPEG compression. This is the canonical source — a palette extractor can only ever approximate what the SVG already states verbatim.

Raster logos (PNG, JPG)

For PNG logos with a transparent background, the extraction is reliable: there are usually only two to four distinct colors and they're flat fills, so any decent algorithm gets them right. For JPG logos, beware of compression artifacts — the edges blur into halo colors that the extractor may rank as "dominant" when they're really just JPEG noise. Crop tightly around the logo art before extracting, ignoring whitespace.

Photos

Photos are the trickiest case because there is no "correct" palette — the algorithm is making aesthetic choices on your behalf. A 4000×3000 sunset photo contains tens of thousands of distinct pixel values; the tool has to cluster them down to five or six. Two good tools will produce different palettes from the same photo, and both can be right.

UI screenshots

Screenshots are deceptively hard. The header gradient, the body background, the card surface, and the elevated modal background can all be within five percent of each other in lightness, and the extractor will collapse them into one color. For UI references, manually eyedropper the four to six anchor surfaces (page bg, card bg, elevated bg, border, primary text, accent) instead of running clustering across the whole image.

Run the extraction

Most modern extractors use one of three algorithms under the hood, and knowing which yours uses matters more than people realize.

AlgorithmHow it picksBest for
Median cutSplits the color space along the longest axis, recursivelyPhotos with broad color ranges
K-means clusteringFinds k centroids that minimize distance to nearest pixelLogos and flat-color illustrations
Octree quantizationBuilds a tree of color cubes, prunes to n leavesFast preview, web tools

For a balanced result on most images, k-means with k=8 then manual deduplication down to 5 is the move. The TinyTools palette generator does this automatically — you upload, it returns five distinct hues plus an auto-generated complementary palette derived from the dominant color.

Field tip: if the extracted palette looks "muddy," your image probably has a global tint (warm/cool color cast from white balance). Run a quick auto-levels in any image editor before extracting and you'll usually get cleaner clusters.

Deduplicate near-identical hues

Eight extracted colors often look like five distinct ones plus three duplicates. The duplicates aren't bugs — they reflect that the algorithm found multiple sub-clusters within what your eye reads as one color (think: shadowed teal vs. lit teal in the same photo). For a brand palette you want one of each.

The rule of thumb: any two colors within ΔE 10 of each other in CIELAB space are perceptually similar enough to merge. You don't need to compute ΔE by hand — most palette tools have a "merge similar" toggle. If yours doesn't, eyeball it: hold your hand over the screen, blur your vision slightly, and any pair that looks like one color is one color.

Fix the accessibility problems early

An extracted palette is a starting point, not a finished design system. Run two checks before you commit:

  1. Contrast pairs. Test every text-on-background combination you'll actually use. WCAG 2.2 still requires 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text and UI components. A palette where the brand color is a mid-tone purple frequently fails as button text on a white background — you'll need a darker or lighter variant.
  2. Color-blind safety. Run the palette through a deuteranopia simulator (the most common form). If your "primary" green and "secondary" red collapse into the same olive, your charts and status indicators will mislead 8% of male users.

If a contrast pair fails, don't throw the palette out — generate a darker and lighter shade of the offending color (a 9-step tint/shade ramp is the modern standard) and use whichever step passes. This is exactly what Tailwind's 50–950 scales are for.

Ship the palette as code

The last step that 70% of designers skip: turn the palette into the format your codebase actually uses. A Notion page full of swatches doesn't ship — CSS variables in :root ship.

:root {
  --color-bg:        #0a0a0f;
  --color-surface:   #15151f;
  --color-elevated:  #1d1d2a;
  --color-border:    #2a2a3a;
  --color-text:      #f5f5fa;
  --color-text-dim:  #a0a0b8;
  --color-primary:   #a855f7;
  --color-accent:    #ec4899;
}

Or as a Tailwind v4 theme block:

@theme {
  --color-brand-50:  #faf5ff;
  --color-brand-500: #a855f7;
  --color-brand-900: #581c87;
}

Whichever format you ship, name the tokens by role (--color-primary) not by hue (--color-purple). The whole point of tokens is that the hex changes and the role doesn't.

The five-minute workflow, end to end

  1. Pick the source image and crop tightly around the relevant area.
  2. If it's a vector logo, grep the SVG. Done.
  3. Otherwise, drop into a palette extractor with k=8 clustering.
  4. Merge any pairs within ΔE 10. You should land at 5 distinct colors.
  5. Run contrast and color-blind checks on the pairs you'll actually ship.
  6. Generate 9-step tint/shade ramps for the primary and accent.
  7. Export as CSS variables or Tailwind theme — name by role, not hue.

That's the entire workflow. Done correctly, you'll go from "I love this photo" to a working brand token file in less time than it takes to make a coffee.

Common questions

Can AI tools like ChatGPT extract a palette from an uploaded image?

They can describe one ("warm terracotta, sage green, cream") but the hex values they return are usually wrong by 10–30 units in each channel — they're guessing from a verbal description, not measuring pixels. Always verify with a real extractor.

What's the right number of colors in a brand palette?

Most modern brand systems land on 5–7 named colors plus 9-step ramps for the primary and accent. Below 5 and you can't differentiate states (success, warning, error, info); above 7 and you'll struggle to remember which one is the "right" gray.

Should I match my competitor's palette?

Match the structure (one bold accent, one neutral system, one or two functional colors), not the hues. If the entire category is using cobalt blue, your cobalt blue is invisible. The fastest way to look distinct is to pick the second-best color in the category and own it — see Linear (purple in a sea of blue dev tools) and Vercel (black-and-white in a sea of gradients).

How do I keep an extracted palette consistent across light and dark mode?

Extract once, then derive the dark-mode counterparts by inverting lightness while preserving hue and saturation in HSL space. hsl(265, 89%, 65%) in light mode becomes hsl(265, 89%, 55%) in dark mode — same brand, different surface. Don't extract two separate palettes from light and dark reference images; you'll end up with a system that drifts.
