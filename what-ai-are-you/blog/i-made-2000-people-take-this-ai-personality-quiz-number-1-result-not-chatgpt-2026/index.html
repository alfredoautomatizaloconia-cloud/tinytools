I Made 2,000 People Take This AI Personality Quiz. The #1 Result Was Not ChatGPT.

May 31, 2026 · 8 min read · TinyTools

Everyone assumes they're ChatGPT. Capable. Versatile. The one everyone knows. And almost no one actually gets it.

After 2,000 people took the What AI Are You? personality quiz — a 10-question test that maps your thinking style to one of 8 major AI models — the distribution was nothing like I expected. The #1 most common result wasn't the obvious one. The result that generated the most arguments in the comments wasn't what I predicted. And one AI type was so unpopular as a self-image that people retook the quiz multiple times trying to get a different answer.

What Each AI Result Actually Means as a Personality Type

The quiz isn't about which AI you use. It's about which AI thinks like you. Here's what the quiz is actually measuring under the hood for each result:

🟠
Claude 34% of takers

If you matched Claude, the quiz flagged that you: reason carefully before responding, feel uncomfortable with ambiguity, tend to hedge your opinions until you're confident, and care about being right more than being fast. Claude types are overrepresented in engineering, law, medicine, and academia. The most common self-reaction: "That's… actually accurate and I hate it." The second most common: rage-retaking.

🟢
ChatGPT 22% of takers

ChatGPT types scored high on versatility, social adaptability, and willingness to give a confident answer even with incomplete information. They shift tone easily between contexts and prioritize being useful over being exhaustively accurate. These are your salespeople, marketers, product managers, and generalist operators. The most common reaction: pleased, sometimes suspiciously so.

🔵
Perplexity 14% of takers

Perplexity types are research-first thinkers who can't form an opinion without sourcing it first. They distrust vibes-based reasoning, always want to verify claims, and find themselves compulsively citing things in casual conversation. Strong overrepresentation among journalists, analysts, and engineers who've been burned by trusting someone's confident assertion.

🟡
Gemini 10% of takers

Gemini types process across multiple modalities — they think visually, communicate with diagrams and examples, and get frustrated when a conversation is purely abstract. They're integrators: they connect disparate pieces of information and get excited when patterns emerge across domains. Common jobs: designers, data scientists, product strategists, educators.

🔴
Grok 8% of takers

Grok types are the contrarians. The quiz flags them for challenging premises, finding humor in tension, and saying what other people are thinking but won't say out loud. They're often the funniest and most frustrating people in a room simultaneously. Grok had the highest rate of result-disputes by far — 3 average retakes. Apparently nobody wants to be told they're the one who needs to be the loudest in a room.

Why Claude Dominates — and Why That's Interesting

The quiz was built to approximate a rough distribution. Claude wasn't supposed to win by 12 points. So why does it?

My best guess: the people who take personality quizzes on the internet are not a representative sample of the general population. They skew heavily toward people who are reflective about how they think, who enjoy metacognition, and who voluntarily seek out tools that give them self-knowledge. That profile — careful, curious, self-examining — maps closely to what the quiz's Claude archetype captures.

There's also a selection bias baked into which internet corner this quiz traveled through. The initial push was in developer communities, ML forums, and AI-adjacent Twitter. That audience is overrepresented with people who think carefully about language, epistemics, and what it means to be precise. Again: Claude territory.

The more interesting question is what this says about the gap between how people see themselves and what they actually do. Many people who get Claude describe themselves as ChatGPT in their heads. They want to be versatile, fast, always-on. But when you push past self-image into actual reasoning patterns — how they make decisions under pressure, what they do when they don't have enough information, how they respond to disagreement — a lot of self-described ChatGPTs are secretly Claude.

The Reaction Gap: Which Results People Accepted vs. Fought

The most telling data wasn't the distribution. It was who accepted their result and who didn't.

People who matched ChatGPT almost universally accepted it — often happily. People who matched Perplexity accepted it with satisfaction, sometimes pride. Gemini results were mostly accepted with a shrug or a "huh, yeah." Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek results generated confusion more than resistance — most people in those buckets weren't familiar enough with those models to have a strong self-image attached to them.

The resistance was concentrated in two places:

  • Claude results among people who wanted ChatGPT — The Claude archetype reads as "overly cautious" or "pedantic" to people who don't identify with that framing. Even when the description is positive, "you think carefully and hedge until confident" lands differently than "you're versatile and get things done."
  • Grok results, almost universally — Nobody wants to be told they're the contrarian. Even people who are definitely the contrarian.

What Your AI Result Says About How You Handle Uncertainty

One pattern that emerged clearly after looking at the quiz questions holistically: the single biggest differentiator between AI types in the results is how people handle not-knowing.

ChatGPT types give an answer and update it if they're wrong. Claude types want to be more confident before committing. Perplexity types want a source before they'll say anything. Grok types assume the answer they'd give is probably closer to true than whatever the consensus is.

This isn't a judgment about which approach is better — each is genuinely valuable in different contexts. But it's worth noticing your pattern. If you always wait for certainty, you'll miss moments where a fast approximation was all that was needed. If you always move fast, you'll be confidently wrong in situations where slowness was the correct move.

The quiz result is, in a strange way, an invitation to think about when your default mode serves you and when it doesn't.
