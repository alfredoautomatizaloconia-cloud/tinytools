What "good" looks like for an indie SaaS domain

The sub-100-character checklist that separates names you will keep from names you will quietly migrate away from in six months:

  • Under 10 characters where possible. Stripe (6), Linear (6), Resend (6), Carrd (5), Tally (5), Plausible (9). Length compounds against you in every podcast intro, business card, and copy-paste forever.
  • Pronounceable on a phone call. If you have to spell it letter by letter to your mom, it is not a brand, it is a license plate.
  • Trademark-clean. Generic dictionary words are landmines. Made-up words like Plausible, Stripe, Resend sail through trademark search. Run a quick check at the USPTO TESS database before you buy.
  • Available on .com, .io, or .dev. Not .xyz, not .tech. Indie hackers earn the right to weird TLDs after their second exit.
  • No hyphens, no numbers, no doubled letters at boundaries. my-app loses 30% of direct-load traffic to myapp.com. Numbers (e.g. 4u) make the name unspeakable. Doubled letters (tooolsforyou) make it unspellable.

Common indie hacker naming mistakes

Patterns that show up over and over in the Show HN graveyard and that the generator is built to avoid:

  1. The "-AI" tax. Slapping "AI" onto your name in 2026 dates the product to the month you launched and pre-loads it with hype baggage. Pick a name that survives the moment AI stops being the marketing wedge.
  2. Hyphenated workarounds. my-cool-tool.com is not the .com. You are paying full .com registration for a domain that will leak a third of its direct-load traffic forever to whoever owns mycooltool.com.
  3. Geographic lock-in. NYCFreelanceTracker reads great until you have a paying user in São Paulo. Pick a name that doesn't pin you to a city.
  4. Spelling traps. Throo, Lytly, Quikz — the kind of name that requires "with two o's, no e" forever. Every single sale becomes an email-correction tax.
  5. Generic dictionary words. Going for journal.com or tasks.com is a fantasy. Premium dictionary .coms start at five figures and the brandable invented alternative is almost always cheaper, more memorable, and trademark-clean.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a good domain name for an indie hacker side project?

A strong indie hacker domain is short (ideally under 10 characters), pronounceable on a podcast without spelling it, brandable enough that it stays usable when the product pivots, and available on .com or a clean .io/.dev. Avoid hyphens, numbers, and double letters at boundaries — they kill direct-load traffic. Most successful indie products use either an invented brandable word (Stripe, Plausible) or a compressed two-word mash-up (Carrd, Tally).

Should I buy .com or .io for a micro SaaS?

Buy .com if it is available. It still wins on direct-load trust and is the only TLD most non-technical users will type unprompted. .io is a strong second choice for developer-focused tools — Plausible, Resend, Mintlify and many others run on .io with no measurable ranking penalty. Avoid .io for any product targeting non-technical buyers or anything in regulated verticals like health, finance, or legal.

How fast should I name a side project before shipping?

If you are pre-launch and pre-revenue, give yourself 60 minutes — not 60 days. Generate a list of 50 candidates, filter to a shortlist of 5 available .com or .io domains, and pick the one you would not be embarrassed to put in a Show HN title. Naming is reversible until you have backlinks. The most expensive naming mistake indie hackers make is delaying ship by two months while bikeshedding the name.

Are weird made-up words good for indie SaaS?

Yes, when they are short and pronounceable. Made-up brandable names (Plausible, Beehiiv, Tally, Carrd, Resend) win because they are trademark-clean, the .com is usually available, and they have no semantic baggage when the product expands beyond the original use case. Avoid made-up words that need to be spelled letter-by-letter when said aloud — those quietly leak conversions for the rest of the product's life.
