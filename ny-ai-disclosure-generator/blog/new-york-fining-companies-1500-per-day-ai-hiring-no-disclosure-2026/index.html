What NYC Local Law 144 Actually Requires (Most HR Teams Get This Wrong)

The law covers "Automated Employment Decision Tools" — any computational process derived from machine learning, statistical modeling, data analytics, or AI that issues a simplified output, score, classification, or recommendation used to substantially assist or replace discretionary decision-making in hiring or promotion decisions.

In plain English: if you use software that ranks resumes, flags candidates, assigns scores, or recommends who to interview — and any part of that involves a model — it's probably an AEDT.

Common misconception: "We use it as a suggestion, not a final decision." The law covers tools that "substantially assist" human decision-making. If a recruiter's shortlist is shaped by AI-ranked output, the tool qualifies.

What LL 144 requires before you use such a tool on any NYC-based candidate:

  • A written notice to candidates at least 10 business days before the AEDT is used
  • An explanation of what the tool assesses (e.g., "this tool analyzes resume language patterns")
  • A statement that candidates may request an alternative selection process
  • An annual bias audit of the AEDT by an independent auditor
  • The audit summary must be publicly posted on your website
  • Candidates must be able to request human review instead

Most hiring teams have the audit checkmark — because the vendors handle it. What they're missing is the candidate notice. It needs to go out before the screen happens. Retroactive notice doesn't satisfy the requirement.

The Three Laws New York Now Has — And Why They're Different

Compliance is further complicated by the fact that New York has three separate AI disclosure frameworks, each with a different scope and penalty structure. Most people conflate them or don't know more than one exists.

Law Scope Required Action Penalty
NYC Local Law 144 AI hiring tools used on NYC candidates Candidate notice + annual bias audit + public audit summary $500 first offense, $1,500/day thereafter
NY GBL § 394-ccc AI-generated synthetic media in advertising Clear disclosure label on any AI-generated ad image, voice, or video $1,000–$10,000 per violation
NY Election Law § 17-200 AI-generated political content (ads, social, mailers) Prominent AI-generated disclaimer within the ad Misdemeanor + civil fines

If you're running AI-generated ads with AI-created images or voiceovers targeting New York consumers, you need the GBL § 394-ccc label — even if you've never heard of it. If those ads are political, you need the election law disclaimer on top of that.

Who Gets Caught — And How

NYC's enforcement path is primarily complaint-driven, which means it follows the candidates. A rejected applicant who knows about LL 144 files a complaint with the NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection. DCWP investigates. If the company can't produce the pre-screening notice and a current bias audit, the violations stack — potentially one per candidate per day.

"We don't need to prove intent. Non-delivery of the notice is itself the violation. Each candidate who didn't receive it is a separate count."
— Paraphrase of DCWP enforcement guidance

The risk profile is lopsided: candidates are increasingly aware of the law (it's in the news), employment lawyers actively look for it in wrongful rejection cases, and the daily accumulation means a company that has been screening without notices for six months could be looking at six-figure liability before they ever get audited.

What a Compliant LL 144 Notice Actually Looks Like

The DCWP published notice requirements, but didn't provide a template. That's created a wide variance in what companies send — a lot of which doesn't actually satisfy the rule. A valid notice must include:

  • The name of the AEDT vendor and tool (e.g., "HireVue Interview Analysis")
  • What the tool evaluates (e.g., word patterns, response time, tone analysis)
  • The data categories it uses (e.g., resume text, video responses, LinkedIn data)
  • A statement of the candidate's right to request an alternative process
  • How to request that alternative (email, form, or phone — at least one method)
  • Timing: delivered at least 10 business days before the tool is used

A vague "we use technology tools in our hiring process" line in a job description doesn't count. Neither does a generic privacy policy mention. The notice needs to be specific, timed correctly, and delivered through a documented channel (email preferred).

The Wider Picture: NY Is the Template Other States Are Copying

New York didn't invent AI hiring disclosure law — Illinois had AEIA before them — but NYC's LL 144 is the most aggressive, most specific, and most actively enforced version of it. It's also the one every other state is watching.

California's AB 2930 (AI transparency in hiring), Colorado's SB 205 (algorithmic discrimination), and federal EEOC guidance on AI employment tools all point in the same direction: pre-use candidate notice, bias auditing, and human review options will become standard across the US. New York got there first and is writing the case law.

What that means practically: the notice workflow you build for LL 144 compliance should be designed to scale. The fields DCWP requires largely overlap with what other states will require. Building it now saves you from rebuilding it in 18 months when California or a federal rule catches up.
