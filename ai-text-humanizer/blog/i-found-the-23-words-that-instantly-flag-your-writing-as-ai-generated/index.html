Why AI Models Keep Using the Same Words

Here's the thing nobody talks about: large language models don't have personal style. They're trained on massive internet corpora and then fine-tuned to sound helpful, balanced, and professional. That fine-tuning creates a house style — a set of vocabulary choices that the model reaches for again and again because they tested well during RLHF.

"Delve" is the canonical example. It was relatively rare in everyday English before 2023. Then GPT-4 launched and suddenly it was everywhere. Academic papers, LinkedIn posts, blog articles, support documentation — all saturated with "delve." AI detectors noticed the statistical spike and started weighting it heavily.

The same thing happened with "tapestry," "nuanced," "foster," "realm," "leverage" (as a verb), "furthermore," "it's worth noting," and about 15 other phrases. They're not wrong. They're just suspiciously frequent — and frequency is exactly what detectors measure.

The Top 5 Offenders (With Replacements)

1. "Delve" → Use "explore," "look at," "dig into," or just cut it. "Let's delve into the implications" → "Here's what this means in practice."

2. "It's worth noting" → This is pure throat-clearing. Remove it. Whatever follows the phrase is the actual sentence. Start with that.

3. "Tapestry" / "Landscape" → These are metaphor crutches. "The evolving AI landscape" — what does that even mean? Say what's actually happening: "AI tools are changing faster than companies can adopt them."

4. "Foster" and "Empower" → Corporate soft-speak that reads like a mission statement. "Foster collaboration" → "Help teams work together." "Empower users" → "Give users control."

5. "Furthermore" / "Moreover" → These are discourse markers that humans almost never use in spoken language. When a detector sees them back-to-back in a blog post, it's a near-certain sign of AI output. Replace with "Also," "And," or restructure the paragraph.

How AI Detectors Actually Work (The Part Most Guides Skip)

Most explainers talk about "perplexity" and "burstiness" without telling you what those mean in practice. Here's the short version:

Perplexity measures how "surprised" a language model is by each word choice. Human writing is messy and unpredictable — high perplexity in places. AI writing is statistically smooth — every word is a likely continuation of the last. Low perplexity = suspicious.

Burstiness measures sentence length variation. Humans write short sentences. Then longer ones. Then brutally long, clause-heavy sentences that trail off. AI models default to medium-length sentences with consistent structure — uniform burstiness.

The vocabulary tells are the fastest signal because they require zero computation — a detector can flag "delve" before it's even finished reading the sentence.

This is why swapping out the 23 words isn't enough on its own. You also need sentence rhythm variation. Short punchy sentences. Followed by longer ones that breathe. And the occasional structural choice that a language model would statistically avoid — starting with "And." Using a one-word sentence. Fragment.
