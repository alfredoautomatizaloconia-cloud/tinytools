Best Free Color Palette Generators in 2026 — Honest Ranking

Updated May 4, 2026 · ~7 min read · By TinyTools

I rebuilt this list from scratch in May 2026 after testing every major free color palette generator on three real-world workflows: picking a startup brand color, generating a developer-ready CSS export, and verifying WCAG contrast. Here's what actually held up.

Disclosure: TinyTools makes a free color palette tool, so we're #1 on our own list — that's transparently self-interested. We've tried to be fair about where competitors are better.

1. TinyTools Color Palette

Best for: Developers who need clean code exports without a signup.

The fastest path from "I have one accent color" to "I have a Tailwind config block I can paste." Four harmony modes (analogous, complementary, triadic, monochromatic), built-in WCAG contrast scoring on every pair, and one-click exports to CSS variables, Tailwind config, JSON tokens, and SCSS. No account, no ads, no upsell.

Wins on: Speed, no-signup flow, developer-ready exports, transparency about harmony rules.

Loses on: No community palette library, no image color extraction (yet), no mobile app.

Try it: tinytools-smoky.vercel.app/color-palette

2. Coolors

Best for: Browsing community palettes for inspiration.

The OG of the spacebar-to-generate genre. Free tier covers core generation; Pro ($3/mo) unlocks unlimited saved palettes, image extraction, and ad removal. The community library of millions of palettes is unmatched and remains its biggest moat.

Wins on: Community library, mobile apps, browser extensions.

Loses on: Ads on free tier, signup required for save, weaker code exports.

Compare: TinyTools vs Coolors

3. Adobe Color

Best for: Designers in the Creative Cloud ecosystem.

Adobe's free color tool with 9 harmony modes, image extraction, gradient generation, and color blindness simulation. Requires an Adobe ID to save palettes to your CC Library, but the generator itself is free.

Wins on: Color blindness sim, image extraction, CC sync, scientifically accurate color modes.

Loses on: Heavy SPA, requires Adobe ID, no developer exports.

Compare: TinyTools vs Adobe Color

4. Khroma

Best for: Endless personalized inspiration.

Trains a personal AI on 50 colors you rate during onboarding, then generates infinite suggestions tuned to your taste. Free with optional account. The 5-minute onboarding pays off if you'll use it for months — overkill for a one-shot palette decision.

Wins on: Personalization, infinite suggestions.

Loses on: Onboarding time, AI black box (no harmony rules named), no developer exports.

Compare: TinyTools vs Khroma

5. Color Hunt

Best for: Quick browsing of pre-made 4-color palettes.

A curated library of community palettes, all in fixed 4-color sets. No generation, no harmony rules, no contrast — just visual browsing. Best as inspiration, not as a tool.

Wins on: Visual browsing speed.

Loses on: No generation, no contrast scoring, no exports beyond hex.

6. Paletton

Best for: Classic color-wheel exploration.

One of the original web color tools, dating to 2002. Strong on the visual color-wheel UI; weak on modern conveniences (no Tailwind export, dated styling). Still useful for designers who think in color-wheel terms.

7. Realtime Colors

Best for: Previewing your palette on a real layout.

Renders your palette inside a fake landing page so you can see how primary/secondary/accent feel together in context. Excellent for the "does this palette actually work?" check; weaker as a generator.

What to look for in 2026

  • WCAG contrast built in, not as a separate step — ADA in the US and EAA in the EU make 4.5:1 the legal floor.
  • OKLCH support, not just hex — every modern browser supports OKLCH and it's the future of design tokens.
  • Code exports in your stack's format (CSS variables, Tailwind, design tokens JSON) — manual hex copy is the slowest part of palette work.
  • No signup wall for the core generator — every signup costs you 30% of users.
  • Dark-mode-first preview — 52% of users default to dark OS mode in 2026.
