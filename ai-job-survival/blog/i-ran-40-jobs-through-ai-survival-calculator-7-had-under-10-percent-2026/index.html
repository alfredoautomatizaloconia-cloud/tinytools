The pattern in this range: the more your job involves judgment calls that require organizational context and relationships, the safer you are. The more it involves producing structured outputs from known inputs, the less safe you are — regardless of how "skilled" it feels to do.

The Product Manager finding: Half the PM's we ran scored under 40%. The shared thread: their survival odds went up dramatically when they had direct authority over the product direction. PMs who are primarily synthesizers and writers of PRDs are more at risk than PMs who own a P&L line or run design sprints with customers.

What connects the plumber and the therapist? Neither can be fully digitized. The plumber works in an unpredictable physical environment where every job is slightly different. The therapist's value is in the therapeutic relationship itself — humans in distress have a documented preference for human support that hasn't budged despite years of chatbot alternatives.

The pattern across the 60%+ jobs: physical presence, unpredictable context, high-stakes human judgment, or legal accountability. AI can assist in all of them. It cannot replace the person who shows up, holds accountability, and navigates ambiguity in real time.

The "CEO / Founder" finding was debated. The calculator scored this at 64% — safer than most people expected, but not invincible. The reasoning: boards, investors, and employees need a human to be accountable. But AI agents are already replacing significant portions of CEO-adjacent work — drafting communications, synthesizing data, running operational reviews. The founder who uses AI well survives. The one who doesn't gets a lower score.

The 3 Variables That Predict Your Score More Than Your Job Title

After running 40 jobs, we noticed the calculator wasn't just pattern-matching on job titles. It was weighing three underlying factors. If you're thinking about your own score, these are the levers.

1. Task Replicability — Not Skill Level

The most counterintuitive finding: skill level and task replicability are not the same thing. A radiologist is highly skilled; reading a routine mammogram is a task that AI has now been shown to do with equivalent or better accuracy. The skill floor doesn't protect you if the task itself is automatable.

The question to ask yourself: could a well-trained AI, given the same inputs I receive, produce the same output? If the answer is yes for more than 60% of your working hours, your score will be low regardless of your experience level.

2. Physical or Relational Context Requirements

Jobs that require being somewhere specific (a jobsite, a hospital room, a client's office) or that require ongoing relationship trust scored significantly higher. These aren't just "soft skills" — they're structural moats. The plumber's value isn't just unclogging a drain; it's being the person in your house who you had to trust enough to let in.

3. Legal Accountability and Licensing

Surgeons, lawyers, and engineers have professional licenses tied to liability. Even when AI can perform the cognitive task, the legal accountability framework hasn't caught up. This creates a transitional moat — probably 5–10 years long — where the human credential is still required for the final sign-off even if AI is doing the underlying work.

Bottom line: The most durable careers over the next decade aren't necessarily the most intellectual. They're the ones that are physically grounded, relationally intense, or legally accountable — and where the human in the loop is structurally required, not just historically assumed.

What Should You Actually Do With This Information?

A 17.5% average survival score across our test batch sounds alarming, and in some ways it is. But it's also a map, not a verdict.

The people in our group who responded most usefully to their scores didn't panic and they didn't dismiss the results. They asked one question: what percentage of my current job is in the safe zone — and can I restructure toward that?

A financial analyst who spends 80% of their time on reporting is at risk. A financial analyst who spends 80% of their time on client relationships and strategic recommendation — and uses AI to do the reporting — looks a lot more like a 60%+ survival score.

The tool is a starting point. The conversation it starts is the point.
