I Ran 40 DevOps Configs Through an AI Disaster Score Calculator. The Results Were Embarrassing.

Published May 31, 2026 · 10 min read · 40 configs tested6 risk patterns1 signal that predicts outages

The average software team has had 2.3 production incidents in the last 90 days. Most of them were preventable. I know because I spent two weeks feeding 40 real DevOps setups into an AI disaster score calculator — and every single high-risk result had at least three of the same six patterns. The calculator doesn't sugarcoat it. Neither will I.

If your team has an on-call rotation, a runbook that hasn't been updated since 2023, and a Slack channel called #incidents with 47 unread messages — read this before you close the tab.

How I ran this experiment

I recruited 40 engineers from Slack communities, LinkedIn, and a couple of "Show HN" threads. Half were from startups under Series B; the rest were from scaleups and one enterprise team that shall remain nameless (their score was a 91 out of 100 on the disaster scale — they know who they are). Each person described their current setup: deployment frequency, monitoring coverage, incident response process, backup posture, dependency management, and on-call load. I fed each description through the DevOps Disaster Score tool and recorded the outputs.

The AI scored each setup on a 0–100 scale. Zero means "boring and healthy." 100 means "this is going to make the postmortems newsletter." The median score was 58. The highest was 94. Three teams scored under 20. I deeply respect those three teams.

Pattern 1: The "We'll Add Monitoring Later" Cliff

High risk — flagged in 34/40 setups

By far the most common finding. The AI flagged it in 34 of the 40 setups with some version of this: "You are deploying to production infrastructure you cannot observe. You will find out something is broken when a user emails you."

The specifics varied — some teams had no APM, some had APM but no alerts, some had alerts that fired into a channel nobody checked. The common thread: monitoring was treated as an optional feature rather than a prerequisite to shipping. The AI called this "optimism-driven operations."

What made this pattern predictive: the 11 teams who reported a production incident in the last 30 days, 10 had been flagged for inadequate observability. The one exception had an incident caused by a third-party API outage — which, notably, they also didn't have an alert for.

"You are not doing DevOps. You are doing Deploy-and-Pray Ops. The difference is measurable in 3am pages."

The 30-second fix

Pick one key user-facing flow. Set up a synthetic monitor that hits it every 5 minutes and pages you on failure. You don't need a full observability platform to stop flying blind — you need one canary that screams before your users do.

Pattern 2: The Runbook Graveyard

High risk — flagged in 28/40 setups

28 teams had runbooks. The AI asked a follow-up question for each: "When was the last time this runbook was tested under actual incident conditions?" The answer was almost universally some variant of "never" or "we ran through it once at onboarding."

A runbook that hasn't been tested is a confidence prop, not an incident tool. The AI was blunt about this: "You have documented the steps to fight a fire you have never actually practiced fighting. Your runbook is a comfort object."

The most dangerous variant is the long runbook. Five teams had runbooks longer than 30 steps for a single incident type. Under actual stress — at 3am, with adrenaline up and Slack blowing up — a 30-step runbook is cognitive overload. The AI flagged anything over 10 steps for a single scenario as a risk multiplier.

Pattern 3: The Single-Engineer Dependency

High risk — flagged in 22/40 setups

This one is the most career-limiting pattern in the list, and the one engineers are most reluctant to name out loud. 22 setups had a critical system — the deployment pipeline, the database migration process, the payment integration — that only one person on the team fully understood.

The AI's framing cut through the usual "knowledge sharing" platitudes: "You have a bus factor of 1 on your most critical path. Your production stability is currently dependent on one person not getting sick, quitting, or going on vacation at the wrong time."

The worst example in my sample: a team of 8 where the only person who could run database migrations was currently on a 3-week sabbatical. They had not shipped to production in 19 days. Their disaster score: 78.

Why this matters more in 2026

Engineer attrition in 2025 ran at record levels across Series A and B startups. If you built a critical dependency on a single person in the last two years and haven't deliberately distributed that knowledge, the statistical probability that they're no longer on your team in 12 months is real. The AI doesn't hedge this.

Pattern 4: The Deployment Frequency Trap

Medium risk — flagged in 19/40 setups

This one surprised me. I expected the riskiest setups to be the ones deploying rarely — the "big bang release every quarter" teams. Those were risky, yes. But 19 setups had the opposite problem: they were deploying multiple times per day with no feature flags, no staged rollouts, and no automated rollback.

The AI scored these almost as badly as the infrequent deployers: "High deployment velocity without blast-radius controls is not continuous delivery. It is continuous gambling. You will eventually deploy something catastrophic at 4:55pm on a Friday."

The combination that predicted incidents most reliably was: deploy frequency above once per day + no automated rollback trigger + monitoring gaps. Eight of the eleven teams with recent incidents had all three. The AI gave this combination a name: the "ship fast, suffer faster" stack.

Pattern 5: The Alert Fatigue Spiral

Medium risk — flagged in 17/40 setups

Seventeen setups had what the AI called "alert desensitization": more than 50 alerts firing per week, the majority of which were either informational noise or false positives that were routinely acknowledged and ignored.

The dangerous thing about alert fatigue isn't the noise — it's what happens to your cognitive response when a real alert fires in a sea of noise. The AI quantified this with a heuristic: if more than 30% of your weekly alerts don't result in any action, your team has stopped trusting the alerting system. At that point you're not monitoring; you're performing monitoring.

"You have 847 alerts this week. Your team resolved 12 of them. The rest were acknowledged and forgotten. When the critical one fires, no one will notice until it's in the logs."

Two teams in my sample had recently had incidents that were "predicted" by an alert that fired 40 minutes before the outage. Both incidents could have been caught. Both alerts were ignored because the teams had been conditioned to ignore that alert class. The disaster score for both: above 75.

Pattern 6: The "It Works in Staging" Assumption

Medium risk — flagged in 15/40 setups

Fifteen setups had significant, undocumented divergence between their staging and production environments. Not small differences — things like: staging using SQLite while prod runs Postgres, staging having no CDN layer, staging traffic being 0.1% of production load.

The AI flagged this with a one-liner that became the most-shared output in my experiment: "Your staging environment is a confidence-building exercise, not a reliability signal. It is telling you 'yes' when production is about to say 'absolutely not.'"

The fix is not "make staging identical to prod" — that's expensive and often impractical. The fix is explicit documentation of the known divergences so that "it works in staging" is never used as a release gate without the caveat "but staging doesn't have X, Y, and Z."

The one signal that predicted outages at an 83% rate

After running all 40 setups, one metric was more predictive of recent incidents than any single pattern: on-call load per engineer per week.

Teams where engineers were being paged more than 4 times per week had experienced a production incident in the last 30 days at an 83% rate. Teams under 4 pages per week: 24%. The inflection point was sharp, not gradual — it wasn't that more pages correlated linearly with incidents. At some threshold, the on-call burnout hits a point where engineers start making reactive, tired decisions that create new incidents.

The AI scored on-call load more heavily than almost any other single factor. If your weekly page count per engineer is above 4, every other number in your disaster score is a secondary concern.

What to do with your score

The three improvements the AI recommended most consistently, across every team type:

  1. One synthetic monitor on your most critical user flow. Set it up today. It takes 20 minutes and it replaces "we heard from a user" with "we knew before the user did."
  2. Reduce your runbook page count by 60%. If it takes more than 10 steps to respond to an incident, your runbook is a documentation project masquerading as an operational tool. Distill it into a decision tree.
  3. Name your bus-factor-1 systems explicitly. Put them in a doc. Then schedule one pairing session per month until two people know every critical path. That's it. You don't need a "knowledge sharing initiative" — you need a list and a calendar invite.
