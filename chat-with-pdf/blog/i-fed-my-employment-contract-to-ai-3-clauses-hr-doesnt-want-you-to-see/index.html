Why Most People Never Actually Read Their Contract

You get the offer. You're excited. HR says "sign by Friday." The document is 40+ pages of dense legalese that was clearly written by lawyers for lawyers. You skim it, sign it, and file it somewhere on your laptop you never open again.

This is completely normal. It's also how employers end up with enormous leverage over employees who have no idea what they agreed to.

A 2024 survey by Rocket Lawyer found that 61% of employees admitted they didn't fully read their employment contract before signing. Among those who did read it, fewer than 1 in 5 said they understood all the clauses. The result? Language that significantly limits your options after you leave — buried on page 34 in a paragraph that starts with "notwithstanding the foregoing."

The fix used to be: hire an employment lawyer. That costs $200–500 for a contract review. Now there's a faster first pass available to anyone with an API key.

Clause #1: The Non-Compete Was Broader Than I Thought

🚨 Clause 1 — Non-Compete / Restrictive Covenant

It covered "any business that competes, directly or indirectly"

My contract defined competitors not just as direct rivals, but as any business "that offers services or products that could be considered substitutes for or alternatives to" the company's offerings. That's a very wide net in a software company.

→ Ask: "Is non-compete enforceability limited in my state?" (California, Minnesota, North Dakota, Oklahoma: largely unenforceable. Most other states: it depends on scope and duration.)

Prompt I used:
"Find every clause related to non-compete, non-solicitation, or restrictive covenants. Quote the exact language, give me the section number, and flag if the definition of 'competitor' or 'competitive activity' is unusually broad."

The AI came back with the exact paragraph, section 8.2(b), and flagged that the definition extended 18 months post-employment and covered any geography "where the Company does or plans to do business." That last part — plans to do business — is the kind of language that makes this nearly unenforceable in most states, but could be used to threaten litigation anyway.

Clause #2: IP Assignment Was Absurdly Wide

⚠ Clause 2 — Intellectual Property Assignment

They claimed ownership of side projects built on personal time

The IP assignment clause covered "any invention, development, discovery, or work created by Employee, whether or not using Company resources, if related to the Company's current or anticipated business activities." The words anticipated business activities are doing a lot of work there.

→ Check if your state has a "moonlighting statute" (California Labor Code 2870, Washington RCW 49.44.140, others) that voids employer IP claims on work done outside company time with personal resources.

Prompt I used:
"Find the IP assignment and inventions clause. Tell me exactly what I assign to the company, whether it covers work done on personal time and personal equipment, and whether there's a carve-out for prior inventions."

I had a side project I'd been working on for two years. Under a broad reading of that clause, the company could argue they owned it. I hadn't noticed because I'd never actually read past the first sentence of section 9.

The good news: I had a prior inventions schedule that I could have filled out on day one. I didn't. Now I know to do that at the next job — it's a one-page list of your existing projects that you carve out from the IP assignment before you sign.

Clause #3: Accrued PTO Forfeiture Was Buried in Appendix B

📋 Clause 3 — PTO / Vacation Payout on Termination

Accrued vacation was not guaranteed to be paid out on exit

The main body of the contract was silent on this. But Appendix B, which most people skip entirely, included a clause stating that accrued PTO is "subject to Company policy as amended from time to time" — meaning the employee handbook, not the contract, governed whether I'd get paid out unused vacation.

→ Check your state law: California, Colorado, Illinois require PTO payout. Most other states don't. If yours doesn't, your PTO balance could go to zero on your last day.

Prompt I used:
"Search all pages including appendices and schedules for any language about vacation, PTO, or paid time off — specifically what happens to accrued, unused vacation if I resign or am terminated."

I had 18 days of accrued PTO. Depending on how the company interpreted that clause, I might only get paid for them because I'm in California. Someone in Texas with the same contract might get nothing.

5 More Prompts Worth Running

Once you have your contract loaded, these are the other questions I ran after the first three hits:

  • Severance: "Is there any guaranteed severance? Under what conditions? Is it contingent on signing anything?"
  • Arbitration: "Is there a mandatory arbitration clause? Does it cover sexual harassment or discrimination claims?"
  • Garden leave: "Is there a notice period, and is there any garden leave or 'gardening leave' provision?"
  • Clawbacks: "Are there any signing bonus clawback provisions? Under what conditions would I have to repay it?"
  • Moonlighting: "What restrictions are there on working for other employers or doing freelance work while employed here?"

What to Do After You Find Something

Finding a concerning clause doesn't mean you can't take the job. It means you have information to negotiate from. A few practical next steps:

  1. Research your state's laws on the specific clause type. Non-compete enforceability in particular varies wildly by state.
  2. Ask to negotiate the clause — most companies will narrow a non-compete, add a prior inventions carve-out, or clarify PTO language if you ask professionally. Asking isn't a red flag; it shows you read the contract.
  3. Consult an employment lawyer for high-stakes items. A 1-hour consultation ($150–300) after using AI to identify the specific issues is much more efficient than a full $500 review of every page.
  4. Document your prior inventions — fill out the prior inventions schedule (it's usually an exhibit or appendix) before you sign, even if it feels awkward.
