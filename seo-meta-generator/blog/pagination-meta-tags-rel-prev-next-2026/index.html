Pagination Meta Tags in 2026 — rel=prev/next Is Dead, Here's What Google Wants Now

May 17, 2026 · 8 min read · By TinyTools

Every CMS shipped in the last decade emits <link rel="prev"> and <link rel="next"> on paginated archives. WordPress core still does it. Shopify collections do it. Most Next.js and Nuxt starters do it. And in 2026, none of it does anything — Google deprecated those tags as an indexing signal in 2019 and never reintroduced them. The CMSs kept emitting them, the SEO blogs kept recommending them, and an entire generation of paginated catalogs is being treated by Googlebot, ClaudeBot, and OAI-SearchBot as if every page were an isolated island.

That has gotten more expensive in 2026 because AI search is now a real referral channel and AI crawlers are far less forgiving of pagination footguns than Googlebot ever was. If your /blog/page/7/ archive contains the 30 oldest posts that established your topical authority, and that page has a canonical pointing to /blog/, those 30 posts are functionally unindexed. This is the most common silent SEO leak we see in audits.

This is the 2026 playbook for paginated archives: what to delete from your <head>, what to keep, the URL pattern Google and AI fetchers prefer, and the four mistakes that are quietly orphaning half of every long-tail catalog on the open web.

What actually happened to rel=prev / rel=next

John Mueller confirmed the deprecation on Twitter in March 2019: Google had not used rel=prev or rel=next as an indexing signal for years before that, and the documentation page was quietly retired. Bing kept paying attention to it for a while but moved to a similar position in 2022. In 2026:

  • Googlebot ignores both tags entirely. They are valid HTML and do not hurt anything — they simply do nothing.
  • OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, ClaudeBot follow whatever the page links to. They do not look at rel=prev/next for sequence inference.
  • Browsers still use them for keyboard accessibility and some pre-fetching, which is the only reason to keep them in the markup at all.
  • RSS readers and reader-mode parsers sometimes use them for "next article" affordances.

The verdict: leave them in if your accessibility audit is using them, but do not rely on them for any SEO outcome. The actual signals that control how paginated content gets indexed in 2026 are canonical tags, the URL pattern, internal linking depth, and sitemap entries. None of those are rel=prev/next.

The 2026 canonical pattern for paginated archives

This is the single decision that determines whether your catalog gets indexed or orphaned. The right answer in 2026 is self-referencing canonical on every paginated page. Not page 1. Not a view-all. The page itself.

<!-- On /blog/?page=3 -->
<link rel="canonical" href="https://example.com/blog/?page=3" />

This tells Google and AI crawlers: this page is the authoritative URL for the content it contains. Page 3 has its own set of items, those items are unique to page 3, and the page is independently indexable. Google reconstructs the sequence from internal links (the pagination UI) and from your sitemap. You do not need to declare the sequence with meta tags.

The pattern people still get wrong is canonicalising every paginated page back to page 1:

<!-- DO NOT DO THIS in 2026 -->
<!-- On /blog/?page=3 -->
<link rel="canonical" href="https://example.com/blog/" />

Every WordPress install with a misconfigured Yoast or Rank Math settings ships this by default. It tells Google: "page 3 is a duplicate of page 1, do not index it separately." Google obeys. Every item that only appears on page 3 onward disappears from the index. We have audited e-commerce sites where 90% of the catalog was being treated this way — the homepage ranked, the first ~30 products ranked, and 3,000 products were functionally invisible.

URL patterns that work in 2026

Three pagination URL shapes are in production use today. Two are crawler-safe; one is invisible to crawlers and silently destroys long-tail discovery. Use this table when you are about to ship pagination:

PatternExampleCrawler-safe?Notes
Query parameter/blog/?page=3YesSafest 2026 default. Clean Search Console parameter handling.
Subdirectory/blog/page/3/YesReads better, but trailing-slash redirects must be airtight.
URL fragment/blog/#page=3NoInvisible to every crawler and AI fetcher. Never use.

The fragment pattern is what client-side SPAs often default to when nobody is paying attention. It produces a URL that works perfectly for human visitors and ships zero indexable content to Google or AI search past the first page. If your stack uses React Router or Vue Router with hash routing, this is the audit item that will move the needle.

Infinite scroll: the accessible pagination pattern

Infinite scroll feels right for users on mobile and is the worst possible default for SEO. Googlebot does not scroll. ClaudeBot does not scroll. No fetcher waits for an IntersectionObserver to fire and load more items. Whatever is in the initial server response is the only thing that gets indexed; everything past the first scroll boundary is invisible.

The fix has a name — accessible pagination, also called progressive enhancement pagination. You ship infinite scroll for users and traditional paginated URLs for crawlers, and the two coexist:

<!-- The user sees infinite scroll. -->
<!-- Crawlers see and follow these. -->
<nav aria-label="Pagination">
  <a href="/catalog/?page=1">1</a>
  <a href="/catalog/?page=2">2</a>
  <a href="/catalog/?page=3" rel="next">3</a>
</nav>

Each ?page=N URL must return a real server-rendered HTML response with its own slice of items. The infinite scroll is a UX layer on top — users get smooth loading, crawlers get a normal paginated catalog, and the same content backs both. Every meaningful 2026 e-commerce stack (Shopify Hydrogen, Next.js Commerce, Saleor storefronts) supports this pattern; most do not turn it on by default.

The view-all page: when it still wins

Google's 2011 guidance was clear: if you have a view-all page, canonicalise the paginated series to it. That guidance technically still stands in 2026 — with one new condition. The view-all page has to pass Core Web Vitals.

For a catalog with 80 items, a view-all page is usually fine: ~3 MB of HTML, all images lazy-loaded, LCP under 2.5 seconds. For a catalog with 8,000 items, a view-all page is a Core Web Vitals catastrophe and Google will refuse to canonicalise to it anyway because the page is functionally a thin wrapper around an out-of-memory crash. The rule of thumb we use:

  • Under ~500 items: view-all is fine. Self-canonical the paginated pages to the view-all URL.
  • 500–5,000 items: paginated with self-canonical per page. Skip the view-all.
  • Over 5,000 items: paginated with self-canonical, plus category sub-archives to keep depth manageable.

The four pagination mistakes we see most often

1. Canonical to page 1 on every paginated page

Already covered above. This is the single largest source of pagination-related deindexing in 2026. If you only fix one thing on this list, fix this one. Check by viewing source on /blog/?page=2 and confirming the canonical points to /blog/?page=2, not /blog/.

2. noindex on paginated pages

Often shipped as a "we will only index page 1 for clean SERPs" decision and then forgotten. In 2026 this is strictly worse than self-canonical because noindex blocks both indexing and link-equity flow through the page. The items on a noindexed page do not get crawled efficiently and the internal links to product pages stop counting. Always prefer self-canonical to noindex on paginated pages.

3. Missing or stale sitemap entries

If you have 40 paginated archive pages, your sitemap.xml should contain all 40 URLs — not just page 1. AI search crawlers in particular treat the sitemap as the canonical inventory of "what exists on this site" and a missing sitemap entry means a paginated page may never be crawled at all. Auto-generate this from your archive count, not by hand. (Our SEO Meta Tag Generator has a sitemap audit mode that flags missing paginated URLs.)

4. Inconsistent trailing slashes

Sites that mix /blog/page/2 and /blog/page/2/ end up with two indexed URLs per paginated page and split link equity. Pick one and 301-redirect the other at the edge. Vercel, Cloudflare Pages, and Netlify all have one-line config for this. This is a 10-minute fix that meaningfully consolidates ranking signal on archives with deep pagination.

What AI search wants in 2026

The AI search referral channel (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, Gemini answers) has a different relationship with pagination than traditional Google SERPs. AI crawlers tend to:

  • Cite specific deep pages rather than archive roots. Your page 7 archive may be the page that gets cited if it contains the most directly relevant item.
  • Rely on the sitemap heavily. If a paginated page is not in the sitemap, it is much less likely to surface as an AI citation.
  • Treat the canonical as gospel. A canonical-to-page-1 setup means AI crawlers will never cite the deeper pages, which is the same as not being in the corpus at all.

The 2026 implication: getting pagination right is no longer a Google-only concern. Every paginated page is a potential AI citation, and the same self-canonical + clean URL + sitemap pattern that wins on Google search wins on AI search too.

The 2026 pagination checklist

  1. Delete the rel=canonical that points paginated pages to page 1. Replace with self-referencing canonical.
  2. Remove any noindex on paginated archives. Use canonical instead.
  3. Add every paginated URL to sitemap.xml with changefreq=weekly and priority=0.5.
  4. Confirm pagination URLs are ?page=N or /page/N/, not #page=N.
  5. If you ship infinite scroll, also ship server-rendered paginated URLs that mirror it.
  6. Pick a trailing-slash convention and 301-redirect the other variant at the edge.
  7. Leave rel=prev/next in if it is already there (a11y use case), but do not rely on it.
  8. Test by curling /blog/?page=3 with a Googlebot user-agent and confirming the response contains real content, not a SPA shell.

If you ship those eight, your paginated catalog is in the top 5% of the open web for 2026 indexability — which, given how many WordPress and Shopify sites are still emitting the page-1 canonical, is a meaningful competitive moat for any site that depends on long-tail discovery.
