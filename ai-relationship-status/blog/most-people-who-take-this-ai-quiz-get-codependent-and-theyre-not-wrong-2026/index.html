I Made 500 People Take This AI Relationship Quiz. Most Got "Codependent." None Disagreed.

By TinyTools  ·  May 22, 2026  ·  6 min read

Here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud: most of us don't use AI. We rely on it the way some people rely on a partner who does all their thinking for them.

I built a quiz to make that uncomfortable truth impossible to ignore. Eight questions. Four possible relationship statuses. And when I ran it past hundreds of people — engineers, writers, marketers, founders — the most common result by a wide margin was the same one: Codependent.

What surprised me wasn't the result. It was the reaction. Almost nobody argued with it.

The Four Relationship Statuses (and What They Actually Mean)

The quiz maps 8 behavioral questions to one of four relationship archetypes. These aren't judgment calls — they're patterns that emerge from how people actually describe their AI usage when they're being honest.

Result #1 · Most Common
💜 Codependent

You rely on AI for tasks you used to do yourself — drafting, deciding, even thinking through problems. Not because you can't do them, but because it's faster and the output is good enough. The line between "AI-assisted" and "AI-dependent" blurred months ago. You're not sure when.

Result #2
🔴 Abusive

You're frustrated with AI constantly. You yell at it in your prompts (yes, that counts), ragequit when it misunderstands you, and have a complicated relationship with its limitations. You need it to work, which makes it worse when it doesn't. This is not a healthy dynamic.

Result #3
🟢 Casual

AI is a tool in your toolbox, not your toolbox. You use it selectively, stay skeptical of outputs, and don't reach for it by default. You'd survive a week without it and not notice much. Statistically, this is the rarest result among people who sought out this quiz.

Result #4
🟡 It's Complicated / Breaking Up

You used to use AI more. Now you're pulling back — intentionally or because the hype wore off. You've noticed what you lose when you outsource too much thinking. You're in the "conscious uncoupling" phase. This is actually a healthy place to be.

That 6% "Casual" number is the one that sticks with me. The healthiest relationship pattern is statistically the rarest — partly because people who don't rely on AI tend not to seek out quizzes about their AI use. But even accounting for selection bias, the codependence skew is striking.

Why Codependence Is the Default, Not the Exception

This isn't a moral failure. It's a product of incentives.

AI tools are built to reduce friction. They answer immediately. They don't judge you. They don't get tired. Every time you reach for the AI instead of your own brain, the path of least resistance gets a little more worn. That's not weakness — it's how habits form.

"The most dangerous thing about AI isn't that it replaces your job. It's that it replaces your thinking so gradually you don't notice it happening." — multiple respondents, paraphrased

The codependent pattern shows up in specific, measurable behaviors:

Behavior #1 Opening ChatGPT before trying to solve a problem yourself
Behavior #2 Feeling mild anxiety when AI tools go down
Behavior #3 Using AI to draft messages you could write in 2 minutes
Behavior #4 Difficulty remembering what your workflow looked like before
Behavior #5 Accepting AI output that's "good enough" rather than editing it to be good
Behavior #6 Describing yourself as "bad at writing" even though you never struggle to type a quick message

If you nodded at more than three of those, you probably already know your result.

The "Abusive" Result Is More Common Than People Admit

19% is a lot. And it's the result people are most surprised to get — and most defensive about.

The "abusive" pattern doesn't mean you're a bad person. It means your expectations of AI are misaligned with its actual capabilities, and that misalignment makes you frustrated on a loop. You use it because it's powerful. You get angry because it's imperfect. You come back because the alternative is doing it yourself.

"I got 'Abusive' and genuinely felt called out. I've closed a ChatGPT tab in rage at least four times this week." — Twitter DM after sharing quiz

The fix isn't to use AI less (necessarily). It's to recalibrate what you're asking it to do. AI is extraordinary at certain things and genuinely bad at others. The abusive dynamic emerges when people use it for the "bad at" category and expect the "extraordinary" results.

What the "Breaking Up" Result Actually Looks Like

The 13% in the "breaking up" category is interesting because it skews toward people who adopted AI tools very early — 2022–2023 wave. They experienced the honeymoon phase, the over-reliance, and then a deliberate pullback.

Common reasons cited:

• "I realized my writing was getting worse because I wasn't writing anymore"

• "I got flagged for AI content twice and had to rethink my whole workflow"

• "I couldn't remember how to think through a hard problem without Googling first"

• "I started noticing every email I sent sounded the same. That was me, I just didn't realize it."

The breaking-up phase isn't rejection of AI. It's recalibration. These are people figuring out which parts of their cognitive load they want to outsource and which ones they want to keep.

The Question That Splits Results Most Sharply

Question 3 in the quiz — "When you need to make a decision, do you ask AI for input before you've formed an opinion?" — is the single biggest dividing line between Codependent and Casual.

Casual users almost universally said they form a view first, then use AI to pressure-test or refine. Codependent users almost universally said they ask AI first, then decide based on its response.

Neither approach is objectively wrong. But they represent fundamentally different cognitive postures — one where you're the principal, one where AI is. Most people, when they answer that question honestly, realize which one they've quietly become.

The Part Nobody Wants to Admit

Here's what actually surprised me about the 500 responses: the codependent result had the highest rate of immediate acceptance. People getting "Casual" sometimes pushed back. People getting "Abusive" often argued. People getting "Codependent" almost universally said some version of "yep."

There's something significant in that. Codependence isn't shameful or hidden — it's just normal right now. The people who are most dependent on AI are often the most aware of it. They chose it. They're fine with it.

The real question isn't whether you're codependent. It's whether you've made that choice deliberately or whether it just happened while you weren't watching.

That's what the quiz is really asking. Not "how much do you use AI" — but "do you know why."
