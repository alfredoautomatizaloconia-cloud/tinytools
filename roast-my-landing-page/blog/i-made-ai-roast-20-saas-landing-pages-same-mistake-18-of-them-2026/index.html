I Made an AI Roast 20 SaaS Landing Pages. The Same Mistake Appeared on 18 of Them.

Published June 2, 2026 · 8 min read · 20 pages roasted18 guilty1 fixable in 5 min

I spent a Saturday running 20 real SaaS landing pages through an AI roaster. Some were from YC-backed startups. Some were solo-founder tools with 3 paying customers. One belonged to a company that raised $4M Series A last March. 18 out of 20 had the exact same problem in their hero section. The founders had no idea.

Here's what the AI found — and how to fix it before your next visitor bounces.

The Setup

I collected 20 landing pages from communities I frequent: Indie Hackers, the YC job board, Product Hunt launches from the past 90 days, and a few referrals from founder friends who asked "why isn't my page converting?" I anonymized them. The AI didn't know which was which. I just pasted each URL and let it rip.

The roast criteria the AI uses: hero clarity, value prop specificity, social proof weight, CTA friction, above-the-fold information density, and something it calls "feature theater" — pages that describe what the product does without ever explaining why a human should care.

I expected chaos. I got a pattern.

The Mistake 18 Out of 20 Pages Made

The AI flagged it differently each time, but it was always the same thing: the hero headline described the product, not the outcome.

Not "you'll stop losing sales to slow follow-up." Instead: "AI-powered CRM with automated workflows."

Not "get your first 10 customers without cold email." Instead: "The all-in-one go-to-market platform."

Not "your next hire will onboard in 2 days, not 2 weeks." Instead: "Streamline HR operations at scale."

The AI's term for this was "feature theater" — pages that are technically accurate and completely useless to a visitor who arrived with a problem and a 6-second attention span. Every single one of those 18 pages described the car's engine specs instead of the feeling of getting there faster.

"Your headline is a press release nobody asked for. Visitors don't come to your site to learn what your product is. They come because something in their life is broken. Speak to the broken thing."

The 2 Pages That Didn't Make This Mistake

Both exceptions were from founders who had clearly talked to their customers. Not guessed. Talked.

One was a small B2B tool for restaurant owners managing shift schedules. The hero said: "Stop losing servers to scheduling chaos — fill every shift in under 3 minutes." No jargon. No feature list. Just the problem, the solution, and a time-bound promise. The AI gave it an 8.4/10 and its only critique was that the CTA button said "Get Started" instead of something more specific.

The other was a Notion-adjacent tool whose headline was simply: "The last place your team's decisions will live." Oddly specific. Instantly clear. The AI noted it was "rare — a headline that makes me feel the pain before I even scroll."

Both founders, when I told them this later, said the same thing: they'd rewritten their headline after customer interviews. One had A/B tested 11 variations over 4 months. The other said he'd read Obviously Awesome twice and finally believed it on the second read.

The Other Patterns the AI Flagged (Across Multiple Pages)

The Social Proof Desert

11 of the 20 pages had no social proof above the fold. Not a logo. Not a quote. Not a number. The AI was blunt: "You're asking a stranger to hand over their email — or their credit card — without a single signal that anyone else has done this and survived." Two of these pages had 500+ paying customers. The proof existed. It just wasn't on the page.

The CTA That Asks for Too Much

8 pages had a primary CTA of "Schedule a Demo" or "Book a Call" as their first and only conversion option. The AI flagged this as "closing the sale before the first date." For tools with a free tier or trial, this is a conversion rate suicide note. The fix is adding a lower-friction secondary CTA — "Start free," "See a live example," or even just "Watch 2-min video."

The Feature List That Ate the Hero

6 pages used their hero section to list 5-8 features with icons. The AI called this "the IKEA instruction manual approach to trust-building — comprehensive, correct, and completely unable to make me feel anything." Feature lists belong in the second or third section. The hero has one job: make the visitor feel understood.

The Speed Problem Nobody Talks About

4 pages loaded over 4 seconds on mobile. The AI flagged this not from a crawl but because I'd noted it when pasting. Its comment: "A slow landing page isn't a technical problem. It's a message to the visitor: we haven't thought hard enough about your time yet." Hard to argue with.

How to Audit Your Own Hero in 5 Minutes

Before you paste your URL into a roaster, do this manually first — it'll make the AI feedback hit harder when you see it confirmed:

  1. Cover your hero section. Read only your subheadline and your CTA button. Could a stranger identify what your product does and who it's for? Most can't.
  2. Replace every "we" with "you." Count how many times your hero says "we," "our," or the product name vs. "you" or "your." If it's more than 2:1 in the product's favor, you're writing for yourself.
  3. The taxi test. Imagine explaining your hero headline to a cab driver in 8 seconds. If it requires product context to understand, it'll require product context for strangers on the internet too — context they don't have.
  4. Find the number. Is there a quantifiable outcome somewhere in your hero? Time saved, money made, headcount avoided, errors reduced? If not, you're asking for trust with no evidence to justify it.

Then paste your URL into Roast My Landing Page and see what the AI catches that you missed. The combination of your own audit + the AI's outside view tends to surface 2-3 things you'd never spot from the inside.

The Uncomfortable Question These 20 Pages Raised

After seeing the same mistake 18 times, I started wondering: why does this keep happening? These aren't amateurs. Several of these founders are sharp, articulate people who can explain their product compellingly in a 10-minute conversation.

The answer I landed on: landing pages are written by people who already know what the product does. The curse of knowledge is brutal. Once you understand that your "AI-powered workflow automation" solves the specific horror of manually updating 6 spreadsheets after every client call, the abstract description feels like it contains that meaning. It doesn't. The meaning is in your head. The visitor sees jargon.

The AI doesn't have this problem. It reads your page like a stranger — because it is one. That's exactly why it keeps catching things you can't.
