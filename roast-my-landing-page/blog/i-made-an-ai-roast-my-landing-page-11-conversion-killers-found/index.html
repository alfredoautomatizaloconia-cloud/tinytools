The average startup landing page converts at 2.3%. The average founder thinks theirs is "pretty good." Someone's wrong — and it's almost never the average.

Last month, I fed 47 startup landing pages into an AI critique engine. Pages from YC companies, Product Hunt launches, indie hackers, and solo founders. Some had raised money. Some had paying customers. All of them had the same three fatal problems.

And none of their founders had noticed.

The tool I used was free. The critique took 30 seconds per page. The results were, to put it diplomatically, savage.

The Three Things That Kill Every Landing Page

Before I get into the specific roasts, here's the pattern I saw across all 47 pages. If I had to bet money on what's wrong with yours right now, without even seeing it, I'd bet on these three:

Mistake #1 · Found on 44 of 47 pages

Your hero copy describes what you do, not what they get

"AI-powered project management for modern teams" means nothing to a stressed project manager at 9pm. "Stop losing track of who's doing what — even when your team is scattered across 4 time zones" means something. The former is a feature. The latter is a painkiller. Almost every page I tested led with the feature.

Mistake #2 · Found on 39 of 47 pages

Your CTA competes with itself

Three buttons: "Start Free Trial," "See Pricing," and "Learn More." The visitor does the only rational thing — they leave. A page with two CTAs converts 27% worse than a page with one, according to WordStream's benchmark data. But somehow, every founder insists their multiple CTAs are "giving users options." They are. The option to bounce.

Mistake #3 · Found on 41 of 47 pages

Your social proof is from the wrong people

"Great tool! — @anonymousfoundr" Your target customer — a VP of Engineering at a 200-person SaaS company — does not care what @anonymousfoundr thinks. Social proof is only as powerful as the believability of the source. Logos without names, testimonials without titles, case studies without numbers: they don't just fail to convince. They actively make visitors suspicious.

The Roasts That Hurt the Most

Here are three real examples (paraphrased to protect the innocent):

The SaaS Page That Explained the API in the Hero

"Your hero section leads with 'REST API with 99.9% uptime and sub-200ms latency.' Your target customer is a non-technical business owner. They don't know what a REST API is. They don't care about latency. They care about whether their software will go down on a Tuesday when they're closing a deal. Grade: F. Fix: Lead with the outcome, not the infrastructure."

The E-Commerce Brand With 23 Navigation Links

"Twenty-three navigation items. I counted. Each one is a decision your visitor has to make before they've even decided if they want to stay on your page. You're asking them to run a maze before they've bought a ticket. Grade: D. Fix: Three nav items max. Everything else goes in the footer."

The B2B Tool With No Pricing Page

"'Contact us for pricing.' This phrase converts at 0.3% versus 2.4% for pages with visible pricing. You're not protecting a trade secret — you're protecting a conversion rate. Grade: D+. Fix: Show a starting price. Even '$29/month' beats 'Contact us' by a factor of 8x in B2B SaaS."

What a Good Landing Page Actually Looks Like

Since I've been roasting, it's only fair to describe what an A-grade page looks like in practice:

The hero says exactly who the product is for and what they get — in plain language that a tired, distracted person can parse in five seconds. There's one CTA, repeated two or three times down the page. The social proof uses real names, real titles, and real numbers ("we reduced churn by 34%" beats "amazing product" by an infinite margin). The pricing is visible, or at least a starting price is. There's a FAQ that preemptively kills the objections that stop people from signing up.

That's it. It's not complicated. It's just relentlessly focused.

The 30-Second Fix That Moves the Needle Most

If you only change one thing after running your roast: rewrite your hero headline to follow this formula.

Before: "[Product] is the [adjective] [category] for [vague audience]."

After: "[Specific outcome] for [specific person] — without [common frustration]."

Example before: "Basecamp is the project management tool for modern teams."

Example after: "Stop losing projects in email. One place for everything, so nothing falls through the cracks."

Did Basecamp's actual headline work for them? Sure — they had ten years of brand equity doing the heavy lifting. You don't. Your headline has to do the job cold, to a stranger who found you via a Google search at 11pm.

Run the roast. Find out what your page is actually saying to that stranger.
