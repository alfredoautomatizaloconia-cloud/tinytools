What C2PA Is (and Why Adobe, Microsoft, and the BBC All Care)

C2PA stands for Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity. It's a technical standard that embeds a tamper-evident chain of custody into any media file — images, videos, audio, documents — declaring how the content was created and what tools touched it.

The founding members of the coalition are not a group of startups. They are Adobe, Microsoft, Intel, ARM, BBC, and Sony. They have since been joined by Google, OpenAI, Amazon, Meta, and roughly 2,000 other organizations. The standard is maintained by the Joint Development Foundation.

The idea is simple: if an image carries a C2PA manifest, anyone can verify what created it. A photo from a camera carries the camera model, GPS data, and timestamp. An AI-generated image carries the model used (Midjourney, Firefly, DALL-E), the generation date, and any editing that happened afterward. The manifest travels with the file and can be read by any compliant platform.

Why This Is Happening Now

The timing is not a coincidence. Three forces converged in 2025–2026 to make content provenance suddenly urgent:

1. The EU AI Act's August 2026 Deadline

Article 50 of the EU AI Act requires that AI-generated content — specifically "deep fakes" and synthetic media — be clearly labeled as such. The enforcement window for this provision opened in February 2025 and the penalty framework for synthetic media disclosure comes fully into force in August 2026. Fines for non-disclosure reach €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.

A C2PA manifest is not the only way to comply, but it is the machine-readable method that platforms can automatically verify. Human-visible text labels ("Made with AI") satisfy the spirit of the law but not the letter of automated platform enforcement.

2. Platforms Are Starting to Enforce It

Adobe Stock's rejection policy is live. LinkedIn began surfacing C2PA provenance data in its "About this image" popups in late 2025. Google Search started labeling images with C2PA-verified provenance in image results. Shutterstock and Getty Images both announced 2026 roadmaps for provenance metadata requirements.

The pattern is consistent: these platforms are building infrastructure to reward labeled content and deprioritize or reject unlabeled AI content — because their enterprise clients (news organizations, advertisers, publishers) are demanding it.

3. Deepfake Fraud Made Provenance a Financial Product

When voice cloning and video deepfakes started being used in corporate fraud — impersonating CEOs on earnings calls, generating fake ID documents for financial onboarding — the financial services industry started lobbying for mandatory provenance standards. Provenance is no longer an ethics question. It has a direct line to insurance liability and fraud prosecution.

What a C2PA Manifest Actually Contains

A manifest is a JSON structure (technically a JUMBF container embedded in the file's EXIF/XMP metadata) with a handful of fields. Here are the ones that matter for AI-generated images:

dc:creator

The person or organization that created the content. This is your name, your company, or your brand. It's also what platforms surface in their "About this image" UI.

c2pa.ai_generative_training

A standardized assertion about whether this content was used for AI training. not_allowed, constrained, or allowed. This is what stock platforms read to enforce your rights as a creator.

c2pa.created

A timestamp and an assertion about how the content was created — in this case ai.generative. This is the field that makes the content "declared as AI" in a machine-readable way.

stds.schema-org.CreativeWork

Optional but recommended: the tool used to generate the content (Midjourney, DALL-E 3, Firefly, Stable Diffusion). This is what EU regulators want to see for traceability.

Signature (hash chain)

The manifest is cryptographically signed, so any editing of the file after signing is detectable. This is the "tamper-evident" part. If you crop the image after generating it, the manifest records the crop as a separate action, preserving the original provenance chain.

Why Most AI Creators Haven't Done This Yet

The short answer: the tools that generate C2PA manifests have been buried inside enterprise software.

Firefly generates manifests by default now, but only if you export from the web app. The Photoshop plugin has it off by default. Midjourney doesn't generate them at all — you have to add them yourself after downloading. DALL-E 3 via ChatGPT started adding c2pa.ai.generative assertions in late 2024 but they're stripped if you screenshot instead of downloading the full image.

If you're generating images in ComfyUI, Automatic1111, or any local model, you get nothing. The entire open-source image generation ecosystem produces unlabeled output by default.

The second problem is tooling. Until recently, generating a C2PA manifest yourself required running a Rust-based CLI tool (c2patool) with a JSON configuration file. The documentation is written for engineers, not creators.

What You Actually Need to Fill In

The minimum viable C2PA manifest for a single AI-generated image requires three pieces of information:

  • Creator name or organization — your name, handle, or company. This is publicly visible when platforms surface the manifest.
  • Generative tool — the AI system used (Midjourney, DALL-E 3, Stable Diffusion, Firefly, etc.). This feeds the c2pa.ai.generative assertion that platforms read for compliance.
  • Training data assertion — whether you allow this image to be used for AI training. The options are not_allowed, constrained (allowed under specific license), or allowed. Most creators pick not_allowed and move on.

That's it. The timestamp is automatic. The cryptographic signature is generated for you. The output is a new image file — visually identical to your original — with the manifest embedded.

A Note on What C2PA Doesn't Do

It's worth being clear about the limits. A C2PA manifest proves what you claim about your content. It doesn't verify the claim independently. You can technically generate a manifest saying an AI image was taken by a Nikon D850 — the signature will be valid, the assertion will be false, and platforms are increasingly using AI detection alongside manifest verification to catch exactly this.

The practical implication: C2PA is not a hack. It's a declaration. Adding a manifest that honestly describes your AI-generated content is a straightforward compliance step. Generating fraudulent manifests to disguise AI content as photographic is a separate category of problem — one that the EU AI Act's disclosure requirements treat as intentional deception.

For honest creators, none of this is a concern. You generated an image with AI. You add a manifest saying so. The manifest travels with the file, and anyone who reads it sees the same thing you put in: this was made with AI, by this person, on this date.
