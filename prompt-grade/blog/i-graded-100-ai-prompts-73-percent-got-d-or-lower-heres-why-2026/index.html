I Graded 100 AI Prompts From Developers. 73% Got a D or Lower. Here's Why.

May 30, 2026 · 7 min read · TinyTools

The average developer prompt scores a 41/100. That means most of what you're sending to GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini is structured in a way that actively works against you — not because you're bad at writing, but because no one ever told you what the model actually needs to produce great output.

I collect prompts. It's a weird hobby, but for the past several months I've been saving every prompt I run across — from X/Twitter threads, Discord servers, GitHub repos, YouTube tutorials, and DMs from developers asking "why isn't this working?"

When I finished building Grade My Prompt — a free tool that evaluates prompts across 22 quality signals — I had 100 real prompts saved up. I ran all of them through. The results were genuinely embarrassing.

And these weren't beginners' prompts. Several came from devs with 10+ years of experience. Some came from teams that have been shipping AI features in production for two years. The grade distribution was almost identical regardless of seniority.

The reason is simple: we've all been trained to give instructions to computers, not to collaborative language models. The mental model is wrong, and that wrong mental model produces consistently weak prompts.

Here are the five mistakes I found on nearly every D or F-graded prompt — and exactly what to do instead.

The 5 Prompt Mistakes That Tank Your Grade

Mistake 01 · Found in 89% of D/F prompts
No Role — You're Talking to "AI" Instead of an Expert

When you don't define who the model is speaking as, it defaults to the most generic possible persona — a vaguely helpful assistant with no particular expertise, depth, or voice. Every response becomes a Wikipedia summary.

❌ What most prompts look like
"Explain how database indexing works."
✅ What an A-grade prompt looks like
"You are a senior backend engineer who has tuned PostgreSQL query performance for high-traffic fintech applications. Explain database indexing to a mid-level developer who understands SQL but has never optimized a slow query."

The second version gives the model a specific expert perspective, calibrates the depth of the answer, and defines the audience — three things that dramatically narrow the output space toward something actually useful.

Mistake 02 · Found in 82% of D/F prompts
No Output Format — You're Getting a Wall of Text Every Time

Language models default to paragraphs. Always. If you don't specify what the output should look like, you'll get running prose regardless of whether you wanted a list, a table, a JSON object, or a one-sentence answer. This isn't laziness — the model literally doesn't know your format preference until you say it.

❌ Vague
"List the pros and cons of microservices."
✅ Specific
"List the top 5 pros and top 5 cons of microservices vs. monoliths. Format as a markdown table with columns: Factor | Monolith | Microservices. Keep each cell under 15 words."

Specifying format isn't micromanaging. It's the difference between output you can paste into a doc and output you have to edit for 10 minutes first.

Mistake 03 · Found in 78% of D/F prompts
No Constraints — "Good" Is Not a Constraint

The most common instruction I see after the main task is some version of "make it good" or "make it professional." These mean nothing to a model. What does "professional" mean? 200 words or 2,000? Formal tone or approachable? Bullet points or prose? Technical depth or executive summary?

"Write a good README for my project."

Without constraints, the model makes all those decisions for you — and usually makes them for the average use case, not yours. The fix is to replace every vague quality word with a specific, measurable instruction.

✅ Better
"Write a README for a CLI tool. Max 400 words. Sections: What it does (2 sentences), Installation (code block), Usage (3 examples with comments), Contributing (1 paragraph). Tone: direct and technical, no marketing language."
Mistake 04 · Found in 71% of D/F prompts
No Context — You Know the Background. The Model Doesn't.

This is the one that trips up experienced developers most often. You've been living inside a problem for three days. You know every edge case, every constraint, every stakeholder concern. You write a prompt as if the model has been in those meetings with you. It hasn't.

The result: technically correct but uselessly generic answers, because the model had to guess what situation you're in.

❌ Missing context
"Help me write an error message for when a user's upload fails."
✅ With context
"I'm building a document processing SaaS. Users upload PDFs for AI analysis. The upload fails when the file exceeds 25MB. Our users are non-technical HR managers, not developers. Write an error message that explains what happened, why the limit exists, and what they should do next. Max 2 sentences. No jargon."

Every piece of context you add is a constraint on the output space. More constraints = better output.

Mistake 05 · Found in 66% of D/F prompts
No Examples — You're Hoping the Model Reads Your Mind

Few-shot prompting is the single highest-leverage improvement most developers never use. Giving the model one or two examples of the exact output you want costs you 30 seconds and improves output quality more than any other single technique — including chain-of-thought, system prompts, or temperature tuning.

If you want the model to write error messages that sound like Stripe's, paste a Stripe error message. If you want commit messages that follow a specific pattern, paste three of them. The model will generalize from the examples faster than it will from a paragraph of explanation.

"Here are two examples of what I mean: [example 1] / [example 2]. Now do the same for: [your actual task]."

That structure — two examples plus the actual request — is worth adding to every prompt template you own.

What A-Grade Prompts Have in Common

The 4% that scored an A weren't longer than the D-grade prompts. They weren't more complex. They just consistently hit four elements: role, context, constraints, and output format. Every single one. No exceptions.

The pattern is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it. Here's a mental checklist you can run through before hitting send on any prompt:

1. Did I tell the model who it is for this task?
2. Did I give it the background context it needs (that only I know)?
3. Did I specify exactly what the output should look like?
4. Did I give it at least one example if the format matters?
5. Did I replace every vague word ("good," "professional," "clear") with a specific constraint?

If you can check all five boxes, you're probably looking at a B or A grade. If you're missing two or more, you're in D territory — and you'll spend more time editing the output than you saved by using AI in the first place.
