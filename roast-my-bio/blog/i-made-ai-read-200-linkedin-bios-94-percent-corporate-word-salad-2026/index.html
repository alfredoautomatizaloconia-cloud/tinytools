I Made an AI Read 200 LinkedIn Bios. 94% Were Corporate Word Salad.

One in seventeen LinkedIn bios earns an A grade. I know this because I spent a weekend feeding two hundred of them into an AI buzzword detector — and the results made me want to delete my own profile.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: LinkedIn has trained us to write badly. Years of seeing "passionate thought leader" and "results-driven professional" in every feed has turned the platform's About section into a cargo-cult ritual. Everyone copies the same phrases because everyone copies the same phrases. And recruiters — who read hundreds of these per week — have learned to skim right past them.

I wanted numbers. So I pulled 200 LinkedIn About sections from people across 14 industries — founders, engineers, marketers, consultants, product managers, and everyone in between — and ran them through Roast My LinkedIn Bio, a free AI tool that grades your bio A through F and tells you exactly which phrases are costing you credibility.

The findings were bleak. And extremely shareable.

What an A-Grade Bio Actually Looks Like

The top 6% shared three traits almost universally:

1. A specific claim in the first sentence

Not "I help companies grow" — but "I've helped 3 B2B SaaS companies hit their first $1M ARR, always starting with sales infrastructure." The specificity signals competence. Vagueness signals the opposite.

2. A number within the first 80 words

Numbers interrupt pattern-matching. A human brain skimming corporate prose will snap to attention when it hits "47%" or "$2.3M" or "12,000 users." Every A-grade bio in my sample had at least one hard number.

3. A human voice — not a press release voice

The best bios read like something a smart, confident person might actually say. Contractions exist. Short sentences exist. Opinions exist. The worst bios read like they were written by a committee with a legal department.

❌ Grade F

"Seasoned marketing professional passionate about creating synergistic brand experiences that drive measurable results and add value across the customer journey."

✅ Grade A

"I run demand gen for Series A–C B2B companies. Last 3 engagements: pipeline 2x'd in under 6 months. I start with the ICP and work backwards — ask me how."

Same industry. Dramatically different signals. The second version took me 30 seconds to rewrite from the first. The difference isn't talent — it's intention.

The Seniority Paradox

One pattern I didn't expect: senior people had worse bios on average than junior people. VP-and-above profiles scored consistently lower than individual contributors. My theory? Senior folks haven't had to update their bio in years — they've been promoted, not re-marketed. Meanwhile, younger professionals who recently job-hunted have spent time optimizing.

The other factor: executives often have assistants or PR teams write their bios, which results in polished corporate language that sounds exactly like what it is — written by someone who doesn't know the person well.

How to Fix Your Bio in 10 Minutes

You don't need a copywriter. You need a framework:

  1. Lead with what you actually do, not how you feel about it. Cut "passionate about" from sentence one. Replace with an active verb and a scope.
  2. Add one number. Any number. Users, revenue, growth percentage, years, team size. Numbers are the antidote to buzzwords.
  3. End with something that invites contact. Not "open to opportunities" (passive) — something like "building something in [space]? Let's talk."
  4. Run it through the AI. The tool will catch the buzzwords you've gone blind to because you wrote them yourself.

The Bigger Problem LinkedIn Has Created

LinkedIn's algorithm rewards engagement, and for years the highest-engaging content has been corporate-humble-brag with buzzword garnish. The platform has effectively trained its users to write in a dialect that communicates nothing useful to the people they're trying to impress.

Recruiters — the people who actually read these bios at scale — know this. Every recruiter I've spoken to says they filter for signals of concrete accomplishment, not self-description. A bio that says "drove $4M in new pipeline in FY25" gets flagged. A bio that says "results-driven strategic thinker passionate about excellence" gets skipped.

The good news: because the bar is so low, standing out is genuinely easy. Get specific. Get human. Run your bio through the buzzword detector. You'll probably be in the top 10% within a lunch break.
