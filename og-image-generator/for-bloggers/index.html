Why your blog's link preview is the most under-invested asset on your site

Bloggers obsess over headlines, SEO meta descriptions, and hero images. Almost nobody thinks about the Open Graph card — the little image that appears when a post gets shared on Twitter, LinkedIn, Facebook, Slack, Discord, or iMessage. Which is strange, because for most posts that image will be seen by ten or twenty times as many people as the post itself.

Here's the math that should worry you. A typical 1,500-word post gets, say, 2,000 organic readers. That same post, if a single person with a modestly engaged following shares it, can surface its link card to 20,000 feed scrollers in an afternoon. The card is what they see. The headline you spent two hours polishing? Cropped or overlaid. The hero photo you licensed from Unsplash? Auto-resized into something fuzzy. The actual click-through to your site depends almost entirely on whether that 1200×630 rectangle looks like it was made with intent.

The good news: making one is the cheapest performance lever you have. It takes thirty seconds per post and costs nothing. Five jobs this generator actually does for bloggers

1. Per-post cards instead of a sitewide default

Most blogs ship with a single OG image baked into the theme. Every post shares it. That image might be your logo, a stock photo, or whatever the theme author chose. The result is that on a Twitter feed, every one of your posts looks identical — the same rectangle, over and over, telling the scroller nothing about what's inside. A per-post card with the actual headline rendered as type is the single biggest visual difference between "this looks like a real publication" and "this looks like a template blog from 2019."

2. Headline A/B testing across social platforms

Bloggers who care about traffic know the headline on their post is rarely the headline that performs best on social. Generate two or three card variants with different framings — a question, a hot-take, a number — and post each from a different account or scheduled slot. The card with the highest click-through tells you which framing to lead with in your next post.

3. Roundup, listicle, and series cards

If you publish series content ("The 7 Best…", "10 Things I Wish I Knew About…", weekly roundups), you want a card system where the number, the topic, and the issue date can vary while the underlying layout stays consistent. Build the template once, swap copy per post, and your archive starts to feel like a magazine instead of a content farm.

4. Newsletter handoff

Bloggers who cross-post to a newsletter platform (Substack, Beehiiv, ConvertKit, Ghost newsletter) need an image that looks good both as an inline header and as the email's social preview when subscribers share the issue. A 1200×630 PNG drops cleanly into both spots. Same file, two channels, zero additional design work.

5. Affiliate and sponsored post cards

When a sponsor or affiliate partner asks for a "promo image" for the post you wrote about their product, they almost always mean an OG card — the thing that will appear in their own social posts pointing back to your site. Hand them a 1200×630 PNG that includes the post title and a tasteful credit to your blog. They get the asset they need; you get a branded share lane back to your domain.

How to wire the image into your blog

WordPress

Install Yoast SEO or Rank Math. In the post editor sidebar, scroll to the social section and upload the PNG as the Facebook/Twitter image. Both plugins write the correct og:image and twitter:image meta tags into the rendered HTML, with the right dimension hints.

Ghost

Upload the image as the post's Feature Image in the post settings panel. Ghost automatically pulls the feature image into the og:image meta tag for that post. No plugin or theme edit required.

Substack and Beehiiv

Set the image as the post's header image. Both platforms expose it as the social preview when a subscriber forwards the email or when the public post URL is shared.

Static site generators (Astro, Hugo, Eleventy)

Save the PNG into your post's bundle folder or static images directory, then reference it in the post's frontmatter under a field your layout already reads (commonly image, cover, or ogImage). The layout's <head> block should already pipe that field into the og:image and twitter:image meta tags — if it doesn't, the official Astro recipe covers the wiring in about ten lines.

What a good blog OG card actually looks like

A few patterns hold up consistently for blog content:

  • The headline takes 60-70% of the canvas. Not your logo. Not your photo. The headline.
  • Type is set at 56-72px, in a sans-serif that survives WebP re-encoding. Hairlines and serif terminals get crushed on LinkedIn and iMessage.
  • Background is solid color or a single soft gradient. No stock photography behind type — it always fights the headline at small sizes.
  • Your site name and (optionally) author handle sit in the bottom-left or bottom-right at 24-28px, dimmed against the background. They're context, not the message.
  • Color contrast hits at least 7:1 between text and background. This is also a basic accessibility floor — see the WCAG 2.2 contrast guidance.

Avoid the temptation to cram in a tagline, a CTA, three icons, and a QR code. The card has roughly two seconds of attention on a feed scroll. One headline. One brand cue. Done.

Frequently asked questions

What size should a blog OG image be?

1200×630 pixels at 1.91:1 is the safe baseline across every major platform in 2026. If you want crisp output on Retina displays after platform-side WebP re-encoding, design at 2400×1260 and let the platform downscale.

Do I need a different image for Twitter vs LinkedIn vs Facebook?

No. A single 1200×630 PNG covers Twitter's summary_large_image card, LinkedIn's preview, Facebook's link card, Slack and Discord unfurls, and iMessage rich previews. Some bloggers also create a 1080×1080 square version for Instagram and Threads, but it's optional.

How fast is "fast enough" for the file?

Aim for under 500 KB. The image needs to be fetched and rendered by each platform's scraper within a couple of seconds, and big files sometimes get dropped silently, especially on iMessage. Pure PNG at this size will usually land at 80-180 KB.

Will my old posts auto-update if I generate new cards now?

Sort of. The image will update the next time each platform's scraper re-fetches your post URL. Twitter, Facebook, and LinkedIn all expose a card debugger tool that forces a fresh fetch — useful when you re-skin a popular old post and want the new card to surface immediately.
