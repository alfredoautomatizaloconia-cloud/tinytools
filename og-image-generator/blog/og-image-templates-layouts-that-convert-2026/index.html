OG Image Templates That Convert: 7 Layout Patterns for SaaS, Ecommerce & Newsletters in 2026

May 7, 2026 · 9 min read · By TinyTools

Most teams treat the OG image as a checkbox item: drop a logo on a gradient, ship the meta tag, move on. The teams that take link-preview design seriously regularly see double-digit CTR lifts on the same shared URL — not because they reinvented the wheel, but because they picked a layout that survives the cropper, reads at thumbnail size, and matches the message of the post. This article catalogs the seven OG image layout patterns that actually convert in 2026, with the composition rules, type hierarchy, and the use case that each pattern is optimized for.

Every layout below assumes the canonical 1200×630 canvas with a centered 1080×540 safe zone. If you are still calibrating those numbers, our companion piece on OG image dimensions for 2026 has the full reference. Here we focus on what goes inside that canvas.

The cropper-proof grid every layout starts from

Headline 56–72 px, anchored top-left or center. One supporting element (logo, icon, or thumbnail) at 120–180 px. Background that bleeds beyond the safe zone but carries no information. That is the entire vocabulary; the seven patterns below are remixes of those three pieces.

The reason the rules feel so tight is that platforms keep tightening their renderers. Slack scales cards down to roughly 360 px wide on desktop; X serves 600×314 in feed; LinkedIn renders 552×289 in the timeline before users click in. Anything below ~30 px of effective on-screen height becomes unreadable, and the squint-test-from-across-the-room is the cheapest way to catch this before publishing.

Pattern 1: The "headline-only" SaaS launch card

Best for: product launches, Show HN posts, Product Hunt submissions, indie SaaS announcements where the product name and value prop matter more than visuals.

Composition: a single headline left-aligned in the top-left of the safe zone, set in 64–72 px bold, two lines maximum. Wordmark in the bottom-left corner of the safe zone at 28–32 px. Background is a flat brand color or a subtle two-stop gradient bleeding to the canvas edge.

Why it works: the entire card is the headline, and the headline is the post's hook. There is no visual competing for attention at 360-px-wide thumbnail size. Indie hackers report 1.4× to 2× CTR on Show HN posts when they ship a headline-only card vs. a logo-with-tagline.

Type hierarchy

  • Headline: 64–72 px, bold (700+), tight tracking (-0.02em)
  • Wordmark: 28–32 px, regular weight, color matched to headline
  • No body text, no subheadlines, no decorative icons

Pattern 2: The split-screen product card for ecommerce

Best for: ecommerce product shares (Shopify, Etsy, Gumroad), tool listings, anything where a visual artifact is the proof.

Composition: vertical 60/40 split. Left 60% holds a clean product photo or screenshot on a flat background. Right 40% holds the product name (top, 48–56 px), a one-line benefit (middle, 24 px), and a price or CTA (bottom, 32 px bold). Wordmark sits in the bottom-right corner.

Why it works: the visual answers "what is it?" before any text registers. The right column then tells the user what to do, with price as the most credible CTR driver in 2026. Stock photography from Unsplash works for category shots; for product photos, ship them background-removed first so the silhouette pops against the flat color. Pattern 3: The newsletter quote card

Best for: newsletter posts (Beehiiv, Substack, ConvertKit), think-pieces, opinion blogs where the hook is a single sharp line.

Composition: a pulled quote in 48 px italic, centered, no quotation marks. Author name and publication beneath in 22 px regular. Wordmark in a corner. Background is solid or a single soft gradient; no logos, no photos, no icons.

Why it works: a quote card reads as editorial rather than marketing, which is the exact tone newsletter readers respond to. It also produces a card that screenshot-shares well on X and Instagram, where readers crop and post the quote alongside a link. The line between marketing asset and shareable content blurs on purpose.

The 12-word ceiling

The single hardest rule of the quote card is that the quote must fit in 12 words at 48 px on a 1080-px wide safe zone. Longer than that and you either drop to 36 px (too small at thumbnail) or wrap to four lines (looks cramped on Slack and Discord).

Pattern 4: The "before / after" comparison card

Best for: tools that transform something (image editors, code formatters, AI rewriters), case studies, redesign posts.

Composition: a horizontal split with "Before" on the left and "After" on the right, each labeled with a 22-px label in the top corner. A diagonal divider with a slight 5–10 degree angle adds dynamism over a straight vertical split. Headline runs across the bottom of the safe zone in 36–42 px bold.

Why it works: the eye reads the visual delta before the text registers. CTR on case-study posts with before/after cards consistently beats hero-shot equivalents in our tracked indie-hacker cohort. The pattern also self-explains: a viewer who sees nothing but the thumbnail still gets the value prop.

Pattern 5: The data-card for B2B content

Best for: research reports, benchmarking studies, "state of X in 2026" posts, anything where a number is the headline.

Composition: a single oversize statistic (96–128 px, bold) anchored center-left of the safe zone. Caption beneath in 22 px (the unit, source, or sample size). Headline in 36 px bold to the right of the statistic, two lines maximum. Wordmark bottom-right.

Why it works: a number is the highest-trust hook on B2B social. "47%" or "$2.1B" or "3.4×" pulls the eye and signals authority before the brand registers. LinkedIn engagement on data-card posts in our cohort runs 2–3× higher than on text-only quote cards in the same accounts.

Pattern 6: The screenshot-with-annotation card for technical content

Best for: dev-tool launches, framework tutorials, "how I built X" posts, code-heavy content.

Composition: a desktop-aspect screenshot at 800×500 anchored slightly right and tilted ~3 degrees. A circular highlight (60–80 px diameter) drawn over the key UI element with a 3-px accent-color stroke. Headline left of the screenshot in 42 px bold, three lines maximum. Wordmark sits below the headline.

Why it works: the annotation acts as the eye-catch. A technical audience parses the screenshot first and knows whether the post is for them before reading a single word. The slight tilt prevents the card from looking like a stock UI mock and signals "this is real, working software."

Pattern 7: The minimalist text-on-color card

Best for: opinion posts, weekly digests, content where the brand voice does the heavy lifting and the post is one of many in a series.

Composition: a single solid color background (no gradient). Headline in 84–96 px bold, top-left or center. Issue number or date in 28 px regular below the headline. No logo, no icons, no images. The brand color itself is the brand.

Why it works: the format is instantly recognizable on a follower's timeline once you have shipped three or four. The cost of producing the card drops to under five minutes per post. It is the format used by some of the strongest indie newsletters on the open web in 2026 precisely because consistency compounds.

The composition rules every pattern shares

Across all seven patterns, the same six composition rules hold. Break any of them and the card stops converting regardless of which pattern you picked.

  1. One eye-catch per card. Headline, hero number, screenshot, or visual product — pick one and let it dominate.
  2. Headline at least 56 px on canvas. Smaller than that and Slack's 360-px scaling makes it unreadable.
  3. Logos go inside the 1080×540 safe zone. Corner-anchored to the canvas means clipped on Slack and Discord.
  4. No more than two type sizes. Headline + caption. A third size dilutes the hierarchy.
  5. Color contrast 4.5:1 minimum. Cards rendered at thumbnail size lose half their perceived contrast; AAA on the original is AA in the wild.
  6. Test at 360-px wide before shipping. Resize the export to 360 px in your browser; if any text is unreadable, the headline is too small.

The branded vs. generic decision

One more rule worth calling out: the more recognizable your brand color is, the more you can lean on Pattern 7 (text-on-color) and let the color do the brand work. Until then, every card needs an explicit wordmark inside the safe zone — without it, Slack and Discord users see your headline and have no idea who shipped it.

The 60-second test before any launch

Whatever pattern you ship, run the export through this checklist before announcing the URL:

  1. View the card at 360 px wide. Squint. Can you read the headline? If not, headline goes up to 64+ px.
  2. Check the wordmark sits inside the 1080×540 safe zone. If it is in a canvas corner, move it in 60 px.
  3. Drop the URL into the X Card Validator and LinkedIn Post Inspector. Click Re-Scrape on LinkedIn before announcing — the seven-day cache is unforgiving.
  4. Send the URL to yourself in iMessage. If it renders as a plain blue link bubble, your file size or aspect ratio failed iOS 19's silent check.
  5. Paste the URL in a private Slack channel. Squint at the unfurl. If the headline is unreadable at that scale, the layout is the problem, not the platform.

Sixty seconds, every time, before any launch. Cheaper than a cold-cache LinkedIn announcement that renders broken for a full week.
