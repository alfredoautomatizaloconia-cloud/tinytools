The 4 Failure Modes I Saw Most

Across 30 products, four categories caused almost every compliance failure. In order of how often they came up:

1. Undisclosed AI interaction (most common)

The EU AI Act requires that any AI system interacting with humans disclose that it is AI. This sounds obvious. In practice, 18 of 30 products had at least one user-facing flow where the AI persona was presented without explicit disclosure — typically a sales chatbot, a customer service widget, or an onboarding assistant given a human name like "Alex" or "Sam".

This falls under Article 50 (transparency obligations). It doesn't require a legal notice in the footer. It requires the system itself to tell users they're interacting with AI at the point of interaction. A buried disclaimer on a Terms page doesn't satisfy this.

"We have a chatbot named Maya. She introduces herself as 'your personal account manager'. We never even thought about this as a disclosure issue."

That founder now has an "I'm an AI assistant" disclosure in Maya's opening message. It took 20 minutes to ship.

2. Biometric or emotion-inference features

Six products had features that flagged immediately: AI that infers emotional state from text, voice analysis that assesses stress or credibility, or "sentiment scoring" applied to individual users in a hiring or lending context.

Emotion inference in workplace and education settings is explicitly listed as a prohibited practice under Article 5. This isn't a gray area — the Act names it directly. The founders who built these features were aware of the privacy angle but had completely missed the AI Act categorization.

One of them — a "hiring intelligence" tool that scored candidate emails for "enthusiasm and cultural fit" — had been in active B2B sales in Germany. They had no idea they were selling a prohibited system into the EU's most litigious B2B market.

3. Misclassified risk level

High-risk AI systems (Annex III) include any AI used in hiring, education access, credit scoring, law enforcement, critical infrastructure, and several other categories. The compliance burden for a high-risk system is substantial: conformity assessments, technical documentation, human oversight requirements, and registration in the EU database.

Eight products were in categories that almost certainly put them in high-risk territory. None of them knew. They'd classified themselves as "minimal risk" because they didn't think of themselves as doing anything obviously dangerous. But "AI that scores resumes" is in the hiring category. "AI that grades student work" is in the education access category. Category membership isn't about intent — it's about function.

4. No AI disclosure generator / labeling on AI-generated content

Three products generated synthetic media (images, audio, video) without any provenance labeling. The Act requires deepfakes and certain categories of AI-generated content to be labeled. None of these three tools had a labeling flow — they generated and delivered content with no metadata about its AI origin.

The irony: two of them were marketing tools. They were helping clients generate content for EU audiences at scale, with zero labeling compliance, while charging for "AI-powered content creation."

The Timeline Founders Keep Getting Wrong

There's a persistent myth in the founder community that "the EU AI Act isn't fully in force yet." This is technically true and practically dangerous — because the parts most likely to affect your product right now are already live.

February 2025: Prohibited practices (Article 5) — already in force. Social scoring, subliminal manipulation, certain biometric categorization: illegal in the EU now.

August 2025: GPAI model obligations and transparency rules (Article 50) — already in force. Chatbots must disclose they're AI. Deepfakes must be labeled.

August 2026: High-risk system obligations (Annex III) — imminent. Conformity assessments, technical documentation, human oversight, EU database registration.

What this means: If you sell AI that touches hiring, credit, education, or law enforcement to EU users, you have months — not years — to achieve compliance.

The August 2026 deadline is 60 days away as of this post. Companies that haven't started their conformity assessment for high-risk systems are running out of runway. A conformity assessment for a complex system can take 3-6 months. Do the math.

What "Compliant" Actually Looks Like for a Small Team

I want to push back on the assumption that compliance requires a legal team and a six-month project. For most AI products that aren't in the high-risk annex, the practical requirements are surprisingly manageable:

  • Disclose AI interaction — one sentence in your UI at first contact. Takes 20 minutes.
  • Label AI-generated content — metadata or an on-screen badge. One sprint.
  • Know your risk category — 15 minutes with a risk assessment tool or a lawyer familiar with the Act.
  • Don't use prohibited features — most founders didn't know they had them. Now you do.

The problem isn't that compliance is hard. It's that most founders haven't run the assessment. They don't know which category they're in, and so they haven't taken the simple steps that would put them in the clear.

High-risk systems are a different conversation. If you're in Annex III, you need a lawyer, a technical documentation trail, and a human oversight procedure that you can demonstrate to regulators. The tool will tell you if that's where you land — and what to prioritize first.

The Question I Get Asked Most

"We're a US company. Can they actually fine us?"

Yes. The Act applies to any AI system deployed in the EU regardless of where the provider is based. EU regulators have a track record of pursuing non-EU companies — GDPR enforcement against US tech companies has been consistent since 2018. The EU AI Act gives national supervisory authorities the same extraterritorial reach.

The more practical risk for small companies isn't a direct regulatory action — it's a customer or partner in the EU requiring compliance documentation before signing a contract. I've already seen this in B2B deals: "We need your EU AI Act conformity status before we can proceed." If you can't answer that question, the deal dies.
