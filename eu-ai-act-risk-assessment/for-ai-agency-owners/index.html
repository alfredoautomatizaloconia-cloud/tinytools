Why the EU AI Act Hits AI Agencies Differently

The EU AI Act carves the AI supply chain into three roles: providers (who develop and place AI on the market), deployers (who use AI in a professional context), and importers/distributors. Most AI agency owners assume they're neither — that they're just a services business wrapping third-party APIs for clients. The regulation disagrees.

If your agency builds a custom AI tool and deploys it under your client's brand, you are almost certainly the provider for that system under Article 3(3). That's the most demanding role in the Act. If you operate an AI service on behalf of a client — say, running automated AI outreach, AI-powered screening, or content moderation — you may be the deployer under Article 3(4). Either way, you're holding obligations your contract with the client probably doesn't cover.

The risk tier determines what those obligations cost you. Minimal-risk means mostly nothing. Limited risk means an Article 50 transparency disclosure your team adds in an afternoon. High-risk — triggered by eight specific Annex III use cases including employment tools, credit tools, and anything touching critical infrastructure — means conformity assessments, technical documentation, human oversight mechanisms, and audit trails. Getting the tier wrong doesn't just create legal exposure: it creates contractual exposure the moment a client gets audited and traces the system back to your agency.

🔴 High Risk (Provider Role)

Custom AI Tools You Build & Deploy

AI hiring screeners, credit-scoring automations, medical intake chatbots, or law enforcement-adjacent tools built for clients. You're the provider. Conformity assessment required.

🟡 Limited Risk (Deployer Role)

AI Services You Operate for Clients

Running an AI chatbot or automated outreach on behalf of a client. Article 50 transparency disclosure required — users must know they're interacting with AI.

🟢 Minimal Risk

Most Agency AI Work

Content generation, internal productivity tools, marketing automation with no direct user-facing decision outputs. No mandatory obligations — but disclosure is best practice and a client differentiator.

5 AI Agency Scenarios & Their EU AI Act Exposure

Use Case 1

Building a White-Label AI Chatbot for an EU Retailer

You built a GPT-4-powered customer support chatbot on behalf of a German e-commerce brand. The brand owns the domain; you built and maintain the backend. Under the EU AI Act, you are the provider — the party that placed this AI system on the EU market — because you developed it for commercial deployment. The retailer is the deployer. This is a limited-risk scenario under Article 50: the chatbot must disclose its AI nature to users. The assessment generates the compliant disclosure copy your client needs to display. Missing it exposes your client to enforcement and exposes you to breach-of-contract claims when they find out.

Use Case 2

Running AI-Powered Recruitment Screening for an HR Client

Your agency operates an automated AI screening pipeline — parsing CVs, scoring candidates, and surfacing shortlists — as a managed service for a UK-based company with EU job applicants. This is textbook Annex III: Article 6(2) and Annex III point 4 classify AI systems that evaluate persons for employment or promotion as high-risk. As the service operator, you are the deployer. Your obligations include maintaining an appropriate human oversight mechanism, keeping logs for a minimum period, and ensuring candidates have the right to explanation. The assessment maps exactly which obligations fall on you versus your client under the Act's deployer rules.

Use Case 3

Delivering AI-Generated Marketing Content for a Financial Services Client

You run a content agency that generates AI-written blog posts, email copy, and social ads for a regulated financial services firm targeting EU consumers. The AI itself is minimal-risk for content generation — but the moment that content influences financial decisions (investment recommendations, insurance comparisons, credit product promotions), the transparency and disclosure obligations of the Act may apply to how the AI output is labeled. If your agency's name is on the production pipeline and your client is audited, the assessment clarifies who is on the hook for what — and generates the disclosure language that protects both parties.

Use Case 4

Building an AI Document Automation Tool for a Law Firm

Your agency built and maintains a custom document drafting tool for a Paris-based law firm — it ingests case data and auto-generates contract clauses, GDPR notices, and litigation summaries used by associates. This falls under the Annex III administration of justice category if the AI output assists in decisions that affect legal rights. That's not the same as the lawyers making the final call — assistance is enough. High-risk classification means the law firm needs a conformity assessment, and your agency — as the party that designed the system architecture — may be required to hand over technical documentation that includes your model card, data governance practices, and accuracy metrics. The assessment tells you exactly what to prepare before a client relationship turns into a discovery request.

Use Case 5

Operating an AI Social Media Moderation Service for EU Platforms

You run a managed AI content moderation service for a network of EU media platforms — using classifiers to flag hate speech, misinformation, and policy violations at scale. Content moderation AI that influences what speech reaches users on platforms above a certain threshold is on the EU AI Act's radar, particularly for General Purpose AI transparency and the intersection with the Digital Services Act. The assessment evaluates your GPAI transparency obligations (if you're exposing a general-purpose model), any Annex III angles related to critical digital infrastructure, and the disclosure requirements your platform clients must surface to users about automated moderation decisions.

FAQ: EU AI Act for AI Agency Owners

What's the difference between being a "provider" and a "deployer" as an agency?

A provider places an AI system on the market or puts it into service — this is typically the agency when it builds a custom tool for a client. A deployer uses an AI system in a professional context — this is typically the client, but also the agency when it operates an AI service on behalf of a client. The assessment asks the right questions to determine which role applies to each of your client engagements, because the obligations (and penalties) differ significantly.

We use GPT-4 via the OpenAI API. Does OpenAI's compliance cover our agency?

No. OpenAI bears provider obligations for the underlying model. When you wrap that model in a custom application, add your own system prompts, and deploy it in a specific use-case context, you become the provider or deployer of that application. Your obligations are separate from and in addition to OpenAI's. The API terms don't transfer regulatory liability.

How do I include EU AI Act compliance in my client proposals without scaring them off?

Frame it as a built-in deliverable: "EU AI Act risk assessment and disclosure documentation included." The assessment generates a shareable report clients can keep in their regulatory files. It's a differentiator, not a blocker — most agencies aren't offering this yet, and enterprise procurement teams are starting to require it.

One of our clients was just acquired by a company with EU operations. Does that retroactively change our obligations?

Potentially yes. If the acquiring company deploys your tool to EU users, a system that was previously out of scope may now fall under the Act. It's worth re-running the assessment against the new deployment context — the tool takes 2 minutes and produces updated documentation you can share with the acquirer's compliance team.

What are the penalties if a tool we built for a client turns out to be high-risk and non-compliant?

For violations related to prohibited AI practices: up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover. For non-compliance with high-risk obligations: up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover. Penalties apply to the provider and deployer — which may include your agency. Beyond fines, there's the contractual exposure: clients can seek indemnification if your deliverable caused their compliance failure.

We're building an AI agent (not just a chatbot) that takes autonomous actions on behalf of clients. Is that different?

Agentic AI systems — those that take actions, execute code, make API calls, or modify external systems autonomously — are under increasing EU AI Act scrutiny, particularly around the human oversight requirements in Article 14. The assessment covers autonomous action scenarios and surfaces the specific oversight mechanisms regulators expect, including the ability for operators to override, pause, or shut down the agent during operation.
