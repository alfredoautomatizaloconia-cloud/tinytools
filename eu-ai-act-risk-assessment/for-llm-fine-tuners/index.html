5 Fine-Tuning Scenarios — and What the EU AI Act Says About Each

🏥
⚠️ Likely High-Risk
Fine-tuning Llama 3 on clinical notes for patient-facing Q&A
Healthcare AI that provides information that influences clinical decisions is an Annex III area 5 system (access to essential services / health). Even if a human doctor is in the loop, the model performing medical triage or advising patients directly triggers high-risk obligations: conformity assessment, EU AI database registration, human oversight design, and a full technical documentation file under Annex IV. Fine-tuning on your own clinical dataset makes you the provider — not just a deployer — which means every obligation lands on you, not the base model vendor.
📋
⚠️ Likely High-Risk
Fine-tuning for automated résumé screening or candidate ranking
Employment and HR AI — including systems that filter, rank, or score job applicants — sits directly in Annex III area 4. It doesn't matter whether the model generates a final decision or just a shortlist. If the AI's output materially influences who gets hired, the model is high-risk. You'll need to assess protected-attribute bias in your fine-tuning data, implement human override controls, and ensure auditable logging for each candidate scoring event. Fine-tuning on proprietary hiring data using LoRA or QLoRA does not reduce this burden — it creates it.
💬
💬 Limited-Risk (Article 50)
Fine-tuning a customer-service chatbot for returns and billing
A fine-tuned model that handles order returns and answers billing questions for an e-commerce store typically falls outside Annex III — no credit scoring, no employment decisions, no critical infrastructure. But Article 50 transparency obligations still apply: users must be clearly told they are interacting with an AI, not a human agent. The disclosure must be made at the start of the interaction, not buried in a FAQ. Fine-tuning doesn't exempt you from this; it just means you're the provider who has to implement it.
⚖️
⚠️ Potentially High-Risk
Fine-tuning for legal contract analysis and risk flagging
Legal AI touches Annex III area 8 (administration of justice) only when it influences actual judicial decisions. Contract analysis for private businesses typically doesn't — if your fine-tuned model summarizes contracts for in-house counsel, that's a preparatory task and the Article 6(3) carve-out may apply. But the moment your model is used by courts, arbitrators, or regulatory authorities to assess evidence or apply law, you're high-risk. Always run the assessment tool to confirm your specific context; the line between legal research assistant and justice-system AI is thinner than it looks.
🎓
⚠️ High-Risk if Evaluative
Fine-tuning an LLM for automated essay scoring or exam proctoring
Education AI — systems that determine or influence access to educational opportunities, track students, or evaluate their performance — is explicitly in Annex III area 3. A fine-tuned essay-grading model that produces the final grade (or heavily shapes it) is high-risk regardless of the institution type. Importantly, fine-tuning on your school's own essay corpus doesn't shrink the obligation — it makes your institution the provider rather than just a deployer of someone else's tool, which is a significantly heavier compliance role with full Annex IV documentation requirements.

The Questions Every LLM Fine-Tuner Asks About the EU AI Act

"I fine-tuned a base model on my company data. Am I now the 'provider' under the Act?"
Almost certainly yes. Article 3(3) defines "provider" as anyone who develops an AI system and places it on the market or puts it into service. Fine-tuning a foundation model — even via LoRA with 10M parameters — creates a new AI system. That makes you the provider with full obligations, and the original model vendor is only a downstream component supplier.
"I'm publishing my fine-tuned weights on Hugging Face. Does that count as 'placing on the market'?"
Publishing weights publicly is placing on the market under Article 3(9). Open-source GPAI models get a partial documentation carve-out (Article 53(2)), but only if you don't exceed the 10^25 FLOP training compute threshold for systemic risk. Most LoRA fine-tunes are well under that — but the base pre-training compute of the model you adapted may already be above it.
"My fine-tuned model generates text responses. Do I need Article 50 disclosure labels?"
Yes, if EU users interact with it. Article 50(2) requires that AI-generated text be marked as artificially generated in a machine-readable format. This applies even to minimal-risk systems — the disclosure obligation is separate from the risk tier. If you're shipping a chat interface on top of your fine-tuned model, both obligations stack.
"Does fine-tuning via OpenPipe or Modal make the platform the provider?"
No. Platforms that supply compute and tooling for fine-tuning are not the provider of your resulting model — you are. Under Article 25, if you substantially modify a model, the obligations shift to you. Hosting-as-a-service doesn't transfer provider status; training-and-deployment does. Your MSPE agreement with the platform won't protect you from the AI Act.

EU AI Act and LLM Fine-Tuning: What You Need to Know in 2026

Why fine-tuning creates new legal obligations

When you fine-tune a foundation model, the EU AI Act treats the resulting model as a new AI system — not a variant of the base model. This is the core compliance trap for LLM fine-tuners: you may believe you're just a user of GPT-4, Mistral, or Llama, but once you run supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), or even a lightweight LoRA adapter on your own dataset, you become the provider under Article 3(3). The base model vendor's compliance infrastructure does not pass through to you.

The provider vs deployer distinction matters enormously

Under Article 25, if you substantially modify an AI system, the provider obligations transfer to you. This is not a gray area for most fine-tuning workflows. Training a new adapter, updating weights with your domain data, or changing the model's output behavior all constitute modification. As provider, you are responsible for the full Article 9 risk management system, Article 10 data governance, Article 11 technical documentation, and Article 12 logging — not your cloud GPU provider, not Hugging Face, not the base model creator.

GPAI rules for fine-tuners publishing weights

If you release your fine-tuned model publicly — on Hugging Face, GitHub, or any registry — you may trigger GPAI (General-Purpose AI) rules under Articles 51–55. Standard GPAI obligations apply to any model that is both general-purpose and publicly available: technical documentation, a copyright policy, and a public training data summary. The heavier systemic-risk tier (Article 55) applies when total training compute exceeds 10^25 FLOPs. Most fine-tuning runs are far below this, but the base model's pre-training compute is relevant if you're republishing a derivative.

Training data and Article 10 data governance

If your fine-tuned model is classified as high-risk, Article 10 requires that your training, validation, and test datasets be "relevant, representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete." For LLM fine-tuners, this means documenting where your instruction dataset came from, what filtering or deduplication was applied, and whether any protected-attribute bias was assessed. If you scraped data from the web, your Article 10 documentation needs to address that. If you used synthetic data generated by another LLM, you need to disclose that in your technical documentation.

Enforcement timeline for fine-tuners

The EU AI Act's prohibition rules (Article 5) were enforceable from February 2, 2025. GPAI model rules (Articles 51–55) apply from August 2, 2025. The full high-risk obligations — conformity assessment, EU database registration, post-market monitoring — apply from August 2, 2026. If your fine-tuned model is deployed commercially in the EU and falls in an Annex III area, you have until August 2026 to complete your conformity assessment. That timeline is shorter than it looks when a Notified Body queue is 12+ months.

Practical first steps for LLM fine-tuners

Start with the free risk assessment above to understand your tier. If you land in high-risk, your immediate actions are: appoint an EU representative (Article 22) if you're a non-EU company, begin drafting your Annex IV technical documentation file, establish a logging system for inference events, and engage a conformity assessment body early — Notified Body capacity is already constrained in 2026. If you land in limited-risk, implement your Article 50 disclosure labels and register the carve-out in the EU AI database. If you land in minimal-risk, document why, then move on.
