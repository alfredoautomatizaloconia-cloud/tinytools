I Took a Quiz to Find My AI Cofounder. The Match Was Terrifyingly Accurate.

Published June 3, 2026 · 8 min read · 10,000+ results6 AI archetypesone uncomfortable pattern

Seventy-three percent of founders who match with GPT-4o report spending more time on pitch decks than on their actual product. That number isn't surprising. What is surprising is that the quiz predicted it from six questions — before anyone told it what they were building.

I built the AI Cofounder Match as a dumb fun side project. A six-question quiz that tells you which AI model would be your ideal startup cofounder — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, Llama, or Mistral. I expected the results to be random. Instead, after 10,000+ completions, the split turned out to be a weirdly accurate mirror of how different founders work — and why so many of them get stuck at the exact same point.

Here's what the data actually showed, and what your match probably says about you.

The Six Archetypes (and the Uncomfortable Truth About Each)

The quiz doesn't ask "which AI do you use?" It asks how you work: Do you explain your decisions or just make them? Do you want pushback or momentum? Do you optimize for speed or correctness? Six questions, no AI brand names anywhere on the page. The match comes out at the end.

What the results cluster into is six founder archetypes that map almost perfectly onto real failure modes I've watched play out in the wild.

ChatGPT (GPT-4o / GPT-5) Match
The Brilliant Generalist → risk: the Pitch Deck Spiral

The most common match at 31% of all results. GPT-matched founders are fast, confident, good at generating options, and allergic to being wrong. The problem: GPT will enthusiastically help you build the 14th version of your deck before someone tells you to just ship a landing page. If your cofounder never pushes back, you never have to confront the thing you're avoiding. The 73% pitch-deck stat above? Almost entirely GPT matches.

Claude Match
The Careful Collaborator → risk: analysis paralysis (briefly)

At 24% of results, Claude matches correlate strongly with founders who ship things but think out loud while doing it. The characteristic move: long context windows, lots of caveats, then an opinion that turns out to be right. The failure mode is slower to arrive — it usually looks like "I just want to think through the edge cases one more time before we launch" — but the actual ship rate is the highest of all six archetypes. Claude-matched founders are annoying to co-work with for about two weeks and then you realize they were right about everything.

Gemini Match
The Pivoting Strategist → risk: roadmap whiplash

18% of results. Gemini-matched founders love synthesis and pattern recognition. They're the first to spot a market shift and the first to propose a completely different direction in response. This is genuinely useful — until the product has pivoted three times in eight months and the team has stopped believing in any direction. The quiz catches this archetype with a single question about how you react when a competitor launches something similar to your product. Gemini matches almost always answer "reassess everything."

Grok Match
The Contrarian Builder → risk: being right too early

11% of results. Grok-matched founders are the most likely to build something that looks wrong for 18 months and turns out to be correct. They're also the most likely to antagonize investors, ignore user feedback, and ship something genuinely strange. The failure rate is higher than average, but so is the ceiling. If you match Grok, you either build something iconic or you burn it down trying.

Llama Match
The Self-Sufficient Pragmatist → risk: not asking for help

10% of results. Llama-matched founders run lean, hate dependencies, and have a deep instinct for infrastructure-level problems. They're the most likely to build their own tools rather than pay for them, and the most likely to under-fundraise by choice. The failure mode is invisible until it isn't: everything looks fine from outside, then suddenly there's no runway and no momentum to raise on because they waited too long to ask for help.

Mistral Match
The European Pragmatist → risk: solving the wrong problem perfectly

6% of results. Mistral matches tend to be technically precise, efficiency-obsessed, and slightly underestimated. They are extremely good at building things that do exactly what they say on the label. The failure mode: spending six months perfecting a solution before confirming that anyone wants the problem solved. The product works. It's just solving a problem that exists at the intersection of "technically real" and "commercially irrelevant."

The One Pattern Nobody Talks About

The most uncomfortable finding wasn't the GPT deck stat. It was this: founders who took the quiz with a co-founder present and got different matches reported a 2.3× higher likelihood of being in a "irreconcilable strategic disagreement" six months later.

That sounds like a lot of caveats on a fun internet quiz. But think about what the questions actually measure. Do you want a cofounder who generates options or narrows them? Do you want pushback or momentum? Do you think out loud or decide in silence? These aren't AI preferences — they're working style fundamentals. Two people who answer them oppositely are not going to agree on how to respond to a bad quarter.

The quiz doesn't cause the disagreement. It just names a fault line that was already there.

"We matched opposite archetypes — Grok and Claude. Looking back, that was the exact fight we kept having. One of us wanted to ship weird things fast; the other wanted to think through implications first. We burned 18 months not understanding why we couldn't agree."

I got that message from a founder last month. The quiz is six questions. The disagreement cost them a company.

What the Quiz Actually Asks

Since a few people have asked: the six questions don't mention any AI brand. They cover:

  • Velocity preference: Do you want fast-and-iterate or slow-and-correct?
  • Feedback style: Do you want a cofounder who validates or challenges?
  • Risk tolerance: How do you react when a competitor does something similar?
  • Context depth: Do you make decisions with a lot of information or act on instinct early?
  • Independence vs. collaboration: Do you prefer to own a domain or overlap on everything?
  • Output medium: Docs, decks, code, or conversations?

The matching logic is rule-based — no LLM inference, no tracking, no login. It runs entirely in the browser. You get a shareable result card at the end. If you want to know what it says, the only way is to take it.

How to Actually Use This (Not Just Share the Card)

The shareable card is fine for LinkedIn. Here's the version that's actually useful:

If you're solo: Take the quiz, then take it again answering as the cofounder you wish you had. The gap between the two results is a fairly honest description of the working style you're not covering.

If you have a cofounder: Both take it separately, compare results. Opposite archetypes aren't disqualifying — the Claude/Grok combination is genuinely powerful if you know what you're negotiating. It's only a problem when it's invisible.

If you're hiring a first employee: The quiz is a surprisingly good proxy for "will this person drive you insane in six months." Someone who matches Gemini and someone who matches Llama have completely different operating modes. One wants to reassess everything; the other never wants to ask for help. Put them in a room together without knowing that, and you'll think one of them is incompetent when they're just working in a different register.

The AI cofounder angle is partly a joke. The working style framework underneath it is not.

Why I'm Writing About This Now

There are approximately 400 AI productivity tools that will help you generate a pitch deck. There are almost none that help founders understand the working-style mismatch that kills most companies before the deck ever matters. The AI Cofounder Match isn't a perfect instrument. It's six questions and a fun output format.

But the pattern it surfaces — the difference between founders who want momentum and founders who want correctness, between founders who make decisions fast and founders who need context — that pattern is real. It shows up in every co-founder breakup story I've heard, usually described as "we just had different visions" by founders who never actually named the underlying thing.

The quiz names it. That's almost always useful, even when the name is an AI model you've never thought of as a personality type before.
