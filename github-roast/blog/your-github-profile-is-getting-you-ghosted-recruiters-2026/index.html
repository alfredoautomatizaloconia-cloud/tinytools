Your GitHub Profile Is Getting You Ghosted by Recruiters. I Asked 3 Hiring Managers to Prove It.

May 23, 2026 · 7 min read · TinyTools

Most developers think their GitHub profile is fine. It has repos. It has commits. Some repos even have stars.

They are wrong. And I have receipts.

I asked three senior engineering recruiters — collectively responsible for hiring loops at companies you've heard of — to review 12 anonymous GitHub profiles while I timed them. They made go/no-go decisions in under 8 seconds on 9 of them. On three profiles, they didn't even scroll.

8 sec

Here's what they flagged — and how to find the same red flags in your own profile before your next application lands in front of one of them.

The Experiment Setup

I recruited three people with real hiring authority: a senior technical recruiter at a growth-stage SaaS company (8 years in eng recruiting), an engineering manager at a public fintech company who screens GitHub before every first call, and an indie hiring consultant who works with Y Combinator portfolio companies. I pulled 12 GitHub profiles ranging from fresh bootcamp grads to senior engineers with decade-long histories. All profiles were anonymized by swapping usernames.

I screenshotted their first scroll depth, recorded audio commentary, and logged exactly what they said before the 8-second mark. The results were uncomfortable.

The 5 Red Flags That Got Profiles Killed Immediately

These aren't hypothetical. Every flag below was cited verbatim by at least two of the three recruiters across multiple profiles.

KILL 🚨 Red Flag #1: The "tutorial graveyard" repo list

If your pinned repos include "todo-app", "weather-app", "portfolio-v1", "expense-tracker", or anything with "tutorial" or "practice" in the name — you're signaling you've been learning but not building. Recruiter quote: "If I see four tutorial repos pinned, I assume they haven't shipped anything real."

KILL 🔴 Red Flag #2: Commit history that screams "weekend warrior"

The contribution graph is read like a vital sign. Flatlines followed by desperate bursts of green right before job applications are visible to anyone who looks. One recruiter called it "cramming your GitHub." Said another: "I can tell when someone suddenly pushed 200 commits in two weeks. It's a red flag, not a green one."

KILL 🟠 Red Flag #3: README files that are basically empty

A repo with no README, or a README that's just the project name and "TODO: add description," tells a recruiter two things: you don't think about communication, and you don't think about other people using your code. Both are disqualifying for any role above junior. This came up in 8 of the 12 profiles.

KILL 🟡 Red Flag #4: Zero stars, zero forks, zero public engagement

This one surprised me. Recruiters aren't looking for GitHub stars the way they'd look at Twitter followers. But they are reading social proof. A profile where literally nothing has been forked or starred by others creates a subtle "no one else cares" heuristic that's hard to shake. Even one repo with 12 stars changes the read entirely.

KILL 🔵 Red Flag #5: The bio says "learning to code" or "aspiring developer"

This is instant self-elimination at any level above entry. You're applying for a job. Describe yourself as someone who does the job. "Aspiring" tells a recruiter you're not there yet. Across all three reviewers, this was the single fastest disqualifier — two of them didn't even look at the repos after seeing it.

The 3 Profiles That Impressed Everyone — What They Had in Common

The three "pass" profiles weren't impressive in flashy ways. No viral projects, no massive star counts. Here's what they shared:

  • One clearly real project — something that clearly solved an actual problem, even if small. A CLI tool for a specific workflow. A scraper for a niche dataset. An integration that connects two APIs in a way that clearly someone wanted.
  • Consistent (not perfect) activity — not daily commits, but no 8-month flatlines either. Regular enough to suggest this person codes because they want to, not just to fill a resume.
  • Readable READMEs with screenshots or GIFs — this got mentioned unprompted by all three. If I can see what your thing does without running it, you're already ahead of 80% of candidates.
  • A bio that stated what they do, not what they want to do — "Backend engineer. Rust and Go. Open to remote roles." That's it. That's all it needs to be.

"I've hired people whose GitHub had 6 repos. I've passed on people with 200. The 6-repo person had one project I could actually use. The 200-repo person had nothing I'd deploy."

— Engineering manager, fintech (paraphrased from interview)

What Your Commit History Actually Reveals (That You Don't Realize)

Here's the part that surprised me most. Recruiters aren't looking at commit frequency for effort signaling. They're reading it as a proxy for habit. Can this person sustain output over time, or do they sprint and crash?

The most hirable profiles in our sample had contribution graphs that looked like a city seen from above at night — mostly lit, with some darker patches, but obviously alive. The least hirable looked like someone turned the lights on right before you arrived.

Commit message quality also came up. One recruiter filtered a profile specifically because every commit message was "fix" or "update." Said she: "If someone can't narrate their own work in 5 words, I'm skeptical they can articulate problems in a code review."

3 Quick Fixes You Can Make Today

1. Update your bio right now (takes 45 seconds)

Go to your profile. Delete anything with "aspiring," "learning," or "passionate about." Replace it with one sentence: what you do, your main stack, and one signal of intent (open to work, building X, etc.). That's your bio. Done.

2. Unpin the tutorial repos

Replace them with whatever project you're proudest of — even if it only has 3 commits and no users. A real project beats a finished tutorial every single time. If you have nothing, start something this weekend and pin the stub with a good README.

3. Add one screenshot to your best README

Open your most interesting repo. If the README doesn't show what the project does visually, add one screenshot or GIF. This single change moves your profile from "I'll have to dig" to "I get it immediately" for every recruiter who lands on it.

The Uncomfortable Truth

Your GitHub profile is not a portfolio. Recruiters don't study it. They scan it, in the same way you'd glance at someone's desk to form an impression of how they work. The impression forms whether you want it to or not.
