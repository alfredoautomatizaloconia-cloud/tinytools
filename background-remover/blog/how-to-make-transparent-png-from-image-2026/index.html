What "Transparent PNG" Actually Means

A PNG file can carry up to four channels per pixel: red, green, blue, and alpha. The alpha channel is what people mean by "transparent." It stores, for every pixel, a number from 0 (fully see-through) to 255 (fully opaque). When you drop a 32-bit PNG into Keynote or Figma, the app respects the alpha channel and lets the underlying canvas show through.

JPGs cannot do this. JPG only has three channels — RGB. A "transparent JPG" does not exist; if you save a cutout as JPG, the empty pixels get filled with white (or whatever background color the encoder chose), and the transparency is destroyed. So step one in any transparent-image workflow is making sure you end up with a PNG, a WebP with alpha, or an SVG. Other formats with alpha support that designers reach for in 2026 include AVIF (smaller files, well-supported in modern apps) and the original Photoshop layered TIFF for print work.

When Cutouts Used to Fail (and Why They Don't Anymore)

For the last decade, automated background removers shared three predictable failure modes: hair, glass, and soft shadows. The 2026 generation of segmentation models — the ones built on the lineage that started with U²-Net and ISNet and got refined with synthetic-data training — handle all three. The TinyTools cutout, like most modern free options, will give you:

  • Hair detail preserved at the strand level. Flyaway hair is no longer a halo of background residue.
  • Translucent glass and bottles cut correctly. The model recognizes that glass is "foreground" but partially transparent, and writes the alpha channel accordingly.
  • Soft shadow handling. You get an option to keep the shadow as part of the cutout or strip it.
  • Edges that don't pixelate. The matting step at the boundary uses sub-pixel alpha, which is what makes a cutout look anti-aliased instead of crunchy.

The Four Export Mistakes That Ruin Transparency

Most "my PNG isn't transparent" issues happen between export and the destination app. These are the four traps:

1. Saving as JPG by accident

Mac's Preview, Windows Photos, and a surprising number of "save image as" right-clicks default to JPG. Triple-check the extension says .png before you close the dialog.

2. Flattening on paste

If you copy-paste a cutout from one app into another, some apps flatten the alpha channel during the paste. Drag the file in from the desktop instead — the alpha channel survives drag-and-drop more reliably than clipboard.

3. PowerPoint's compression

PowerPoint will silently re-compress images on save. Go to File → Options → Advanced → Image Size and Quality and tick "Do not compress images in file." Otherwise your beautiful cutout gets re-encoded as a JPG-quality blur with chunky edges.

4. Old browsers stripping alpha on upload

If you're uploading a transparent PNG to a CMS and it shows up with a white background, check whether the CMS is auto-generating thumbnails. Many ecommerce themes still flatten alpha when generating preview sizes. The fix is uploading a separate, smaller transparent PNG specifically as the thumbnail.

The Privacy Argument for In-Browser Tools

One thing the in-browser approach gets right that server-based tools never will: your file never leaves your laptop. Older free background removers upload your image to a server, run inference there, and email you the link. That's fine for memes; it's not fine for unreleased product photos, internal headshots, or anything under NDA. A modern in-browser tool runs the model in your tab — it cannot leak the file because the file never makes the trip.

If you handle anything sensitive — pre-launch product photography, executive headshots, customer-supplied images — make sure the tool you pick says, in plain language, "runs in your browser." If you can't find that claim, assume the file is being uploaded.
