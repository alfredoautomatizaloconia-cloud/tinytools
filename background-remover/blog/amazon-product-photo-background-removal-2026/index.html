Why Amazon Treats Your Background Like a Compliance Document

Amazon's search ranking algorithm folds in a "listing quality" score that nobody sees but everybody feels. The image portion of that score has two components: policy compliance (does your main image meet the spec?) and commercial signal (does it convert?). The first one is binary — fail it and your listing gets quietly deprioritized until you fix it. The second is graded.

The hard part for new sellers is that nothing tells you when you've failed compliance. The Buy Box just gets harder to win. Conversion mysteriously drops. A/B tests on title and price look noisy. The image is the variable they didn't think to question.

The Free 3-Step Workflow That Holds Up Under Audit

Step 1: Shoot with the cutout in mind

The cleanest cutout starts with the cleanest source. Amazon's spec is unforgiving, so the goal is to give your background remover the easiest possible separation problem:

  • Shoot against a contrasting backdrop, not white. A white product on a white sweep gives the model nothing to grab. A mid-grey muslin (or the back of a foam board) gives 2 stops of separation, which the segmentation model uses to find your edges.
  • Diffuse from two sides. A bedsheet over a window plus a fill card on the opposite side eliminates the harsh shadows that confuse cutout models on glossy or transparent products.
  • Lock the camera and rotate the product. Amazon allows up to 7 supplementary images. Shoot all 8 angles in one session against the same backdrop so your eventual cutouts share lighting — buyers don't notice when it's right, but they bounce when it's wrong.
  • Leave headroom. Shoot a little wider than 85%. You'll crop tighter in post when you composite onto white. Cropping in is free; cropping out is impossible.

Categories where Amazon listings most often fail compliance for cutout reasons, and what works in 2026:

  • Glossy electronics: Reflections of the room can be flagged as "props" by the audit. Use the matte-finish toggle to dim specular highlights below the threshold.
  • Apparel on hangers: The hanger counts as a prop in the main image. Cut it out manually — most browser tools have a "subtract" brush. Keep the hanger in the slot-2 detail shot.
  • Glass, candles, perfume bottles: Translucent products are the #1 reason for "looks edited" rejections. Use the preserve-transparency mode and the model will keep the alpha values inside the bottle instead of cutting it flat.
  • Cables and thin straps: Shoot at f/8 minimum so they stay in focus. Modern segmentation handles cables under 3 pixels wide if they're sharp.

Step 3: Composite onto pure white at the right frame fill

The most-failed step of the whole pipeline. A bare transparent PNG on Amazon's white listing background looks compliant, but if you've been editing on a calibrated monitor your "white" canvas might actually be #FCFCFC or #FEFEFE. The audit uses an eyedropper. Anything off pure #FFFFFF can fail.

The fix is two checkboxes during export: "Pure white background" (forces #FFFFFF, not your editor's default) and "85% frame fit" (auto-scales the cutout to fill exactly 87% of the canvas, giving you safety margin). The 3 Mistakes That Get Amazon Listings Quietly Suppressed

Mistake 1: The off-white halo

If you cut a product out of a grey backdrop and paste it onto white, you'll often see a faint grey halo at the edges. Amazon's audit catches this. The fix is a single "decontaminate edges" pass, which the TinyTools remover applies by default. Visually inspect at 200% zoom — if you can see any non-white pixels touching the canvas edge, the audit will too.

Mistake 2: Frame fill under 85%

This is the single biggest reason new listings get suppressed. Sellers shoot wide for safety, the cutout ends up filling 70% of the frame, the listing goes live, and Amazon's image audit flags it three days later — by which point it's already lost its launch momentum. Run every main image through a quick check: does the bounding box of the product cover at least 85% of either the width or the height of the canvas? If not, scale up before you upload.

Mistake 3: Props that look like accessories

Amazon's policy bans props in the main image. The trap is that "props" includes things sellers genuinely think of as part of the product — the case the AirPods came in, the cord wrapped around a charger, the plant in a planter, the candle wax in a candle. If it's not the SKU you're selling, it's a prop. Move it to slot 2.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Amazon prefer JPG or PNG for the main image?

Either is allowed. Top sellers ship JPG at 92 quality because it's roughly 60% smaller than PNG without visible quality loss at typical thumbnail sizes, which speeds the listing page slightly — and Amazon does fold page speed into the conversion side of listing quality. Cut as PNG, composite onto white, then export flat as JPG at upload.

Is it OK to add a soft drop shadow under the product?

No, not on the main image. Amazon's spec specifically calls out shadows as "props" for the main image. Soft drop shadows are fine in slot 2 onward. If your product looks like it's floating without a shadow, that's a sign you need to add a tiny "ground reflection" rather than a shadow — Amazon's policy treats them differently.

What about AI-generated lifestyle backgrounds for the secondary slots?

Allowed in 2026 for slots 2–7, with caveats. Amazon's transparency rules require AI-generated lifestyle images to actually represent the product as it ships — no different colors, no different sizes, no features the SKU doesn't have. The cutout itself is not considered AI-generated; only the lifestyle scene you composite onto needs to meet that "as-shipped" rule.

How do I know if my listing was suppressed for an image issue?

In Seller Central, go to Manage Inventory and filter by "Suppressed." Amazon flags image issues with a yellow caution icon and a one-line reason — most commonly "Main image must have a pure white background." Re-upload and the listing returns within 24 hours, but it loses its prior search position.
