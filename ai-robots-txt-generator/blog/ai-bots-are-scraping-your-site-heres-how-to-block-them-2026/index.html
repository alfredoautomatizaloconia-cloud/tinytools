Why This Matters More Than You Think

The "so what?" reaction is understandable. Your site is public. Anyone can read it. But AI crawling is different from a human reader in three important ways:

1. Scale and frequency

A motivated human might read your 50-page website once. An AI crawler will re-index it every few days, pulling every page, image alt text, embedded PDF, and meta description. High-frequency crawling means real bandwidth costs — especially on hosting plans with usage limits, or sites running expensive serverless functions on every page load.

2. Your content fuels their competitive moat

If you've spent two years writing detailed tutorials, product comparisons, or original research, that content is being absorbed into models that will then compete with you in search results through AI-generated answers. The AI Overviews in Google Search, the Perplexity answers, the ChatGPT replies — your uncredited work is in there somewhere.

3. The legal landscape is shifting fast

The EU AI Act (enforcement escalating through 2026) explicitly addresses training data provenance. Several jurisdictions are moving toward "opt-in" rather than "opt-out" models for AI training data. Getting your robots.txt updated now puts you on the right side of a legal trend that's only going in one direction.

The "Allow Some, Block Others" Strategy

Not everyone wants to go scorched earth on AI crawlers. Here are the three most common nuanced strategies:

Strategy 1: Allow Perplexity, block training crawlers

Perplexity sends traffic back. If someone asks Perplexity about your topic, it may cite and link to your site. Some publishers have found Perplexity is actually a decent referral source — unlike training crawlers that never send a click back. If that's your situation, allow PerplexityBot while blocking everything else.

Strategy 2: Block by path, not wholesale

Maybe you're fine with AI using your public blog posts for training, but you want to protect your premium content, API docs, or subscriber-only sections. Robots.txt supports path-level rules:

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /members/
Disallow: /api/
Disallow: /downloads/
Allow: /blog/

Strategy 3: Selective opt-in for visibility

Some creators are deliberately leaving their content open to AI crawlers, reasoning that being cited in AI answers is the new SEO. There's a real debate here — no clear winner yet. If that's your position, make it an intentional choice by explicitly allowing specific bots rather than just having no robots.txt at all.

How to Check If Your robots.txt Is Actually Working

Writing the rules is step one. Verifying them is step two, and most people skip it. Three ways to check:

  1. Google Search Console → Robots.txt Tester — validates syntax and lets you test any URL against your rules. Free, authoritative.
  2. Your server access logs — search for the bot user-agent strings 7 days after deploying your new robots.txt. If a compliant bot is still appearing, re-check your syntax.
  3. Fetch your own robots.txt — run curl https://yourdomain.com/robots.txt and read it with fresh eyes. The most common bug is a typo in the path or a trailing space after the user-agent name.
