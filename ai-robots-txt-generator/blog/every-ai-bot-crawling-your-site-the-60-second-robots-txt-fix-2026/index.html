And those bots are not bound by the same norms as search crawlers.

Why Your Current robots.txt Doesn't Help

The standard robots.txt that most sites have looks something like this:

# Standard robots.txt - written pre-2023 User-agent: * Disallow: /admin/ Disallow: /private/ Sitemap: https://yoursite.com/sitemap.xml

That User-agent: * directive applies to all crawlers — but only for the disallowed paths you list. Your actual public content? Fully accessible. And every single AI training bot listed above will crawl it freely.

The common misconception is that User-agent: * with disallowed admin paths "locks down" your site. It doesn't. It only blocks specific paths. Your blog posts, documentation, product pages, code examples, written guides — anything publicly accessible and not explicitly disallowed — is being indexed and likely included in training datasets.

The legal nuance: Under the EU AI Act (enforcement began August 2025 for high-risk AI systems), AI companies using web-scraped training data must honor robots.txt directives if they are present. Reputable companies like OpenAI and Anthropic have committed to this. But if your robots.txt doesn't include AI-specific User-Agent strings, your content is considered fair game — because you haven't expressed a preference.

The robots.txt File That Blocks All of Them

Here's what a properly configured robots.txt looks like if you want to block all AI training crawlers while keeping Google Search and Bing accessible:

# AI Training Crawlers — blocked User-agent: GPTBot Disallow: / User-agent: ChatGPT-User Disallow: / User-agent: ClaudeBot Disallow: / User-agent: Google-Extended Disallow: / User-agent: FacebookBot Disallow: / User-agent: Applebot-Extended Disallow: / User-agent: CCBot Disallow: / User-agent: PerplexityBot Disallow: / User-agent: cohere-ai Disallow: / User-agent: Bytespider Disallow: / User-agent: Amazonbot Disallow: / User-agent: DiffBot Disallow: / User-agent: omgili Disallow: / User-agent: ia_archiver Disallow: / # Search engines — still allowed User-agent: Googlebot Allow: / User-agent: Bingbot Allow: / User-agent: * Disallow: /admin/ Sitemap: https://yoursite.com/sitemap.xml

That's the defensive configuration. But robots.txt strategy isn't one-size-fits-all. You might want to allow AI search indexers (like Perplexity) for visibility while blocking training crawlers. You might want to allow specific sections of your site for AI but protect your premium content. The logic branches quickly.

The Decision You Actually Need to Make

Before you copy-paste a block-everything configuration, there are some real tradeoffs worth understanding.

Blocking AI crawlers: the case for it

If you create original written content, your work is being used to train systems that may compete with you or your industry. If you're a writer, journalist, educator, developer, or creator of any kind — your output is a training asset for these companies. Opting out is a reasonable choice, and it's currently your only legal lever in most jurisdictions.

Allowing AI crawlers: the case for it

AI-powered search tools like Perplexity are increasingly driving traffic. Blocking them entirely means your content won't appear in AI-generated answers, which is a meaningful source of referral traffic for many sites in 2026. Some creators deliberately allow AI search indexers while blocking training-only crawlers — this is the nuanced option and it requires careful per-bot configuration.

The middle path most sites choose: Allow AI search bots (PerplexityBot, ChatGPT-User) that can send referral traffic. Block training-only crawlers (GPTBot, Google-Extended, CCBot) that consume your content without attribution. This gives you AI search visibility while opting out of silent training data collection.

What Happens If You Don't Act

In the short term: nothing visible. AI crawlers are polite — they don't slow down your site, they don't trigger error logs that would alert you. They just read and leave. You won't notice them unless you're actively watching your server access logs and know what User-Agent strings to look for.

In the medium term: your content becomes training data for the next generation of AI models. Once your content is in a training dataset, there's no practical mechanism to remove it. Even if you add robots.txt blocks tomorrow, that content is already in whatever dataset was collected before you blocked it. The window to act is before crawling, not after.

In the long term: the EU AI Act's transparency requirements around training data are still being actively enforced and refined. Regulators in the EU, UK, and Canada are building frameworks that may give creators explicit rights over their training data — but those frameworks generally require that you have expressed a preference via mechanisms like robots.txt. Sites with no AI-specific robots.txt may have a weaker legal standing in future disputes over training data use.

If you run an EU-based website or target EU users: The EU AI Act's requirements create additional incentive to have a clear robots.txt policy documented and in place. Check your EU AI Act Risk Assessment to understand your full compliance posture — robots.txt is just one piece of the picture.

The 60-Second Check: Is Your Site Currently Being Crawled?

You don't need to wait to find out. Here's how to check your access logs right now:

If you have SSH access to your server, run:

grep -E "(GPTBot|ClaudeBot|Google-Extended|CCBot|PerplexityBot)" /var/log/nginx/access.log | tail -20

If you're on a managed host (Vercel, Netlify, Cloudflare), check your analytics dashboard for unusual bot traffic — or look at your traffic referrer breakdown for AI-specific sources.

Most site owners who do this check are surprised. GPTBot and ClaudeBot in particular are active crawlers. A mid-size blog with 10,000 monthly visitors will typically show dozens of GPTBot visits per day in their access logs.
